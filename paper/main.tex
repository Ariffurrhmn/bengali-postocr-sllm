\begin{abstract}
Optical character recognition (OCR) of historical Bengali print is error-prone, and correcting its output with a small language model has been suggested as a low-cost remedy that needs no retraining. We test this on 40 evaluation pages of Bengali books printed between 1713 and 1914. Tesseract and EasyOCR produce the text, which eight open models of 0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters correct zero-shot; a dictionary spell-checker serves as a classical baseline. No method significantly lowered the character error rate (CER) of either engine. The best general-purpose models mostly returned their input (Gemma 2B: CER 0.341 to 0.353 on Tesseract output, 0.297 to 0.301 on EasyOCR output), while the Bengali-specific models regenerated the text and raised CER to between 0.83 and 1.64. Every method altered text that contained no error. The only gains were small: Qwen3 1.7B lowered the word error rate on Tesseract output from 0.827 to 0.799 and, with its edits restricted to words the engine was unsure of, CER by 0.004. A repetition penalty that also acts on the prompt had made copying models appear harmful in our first runs, raising the CER of Gemma 2B from 0.364 to 0.583 on a 15-page sample; prompt variants, model-independent chunking and output safeguards did not change the conclusion. The study covers zero-shot use of small models on 40 pages; fine-tuning remains untested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bengali ranks seventh among the world's languages by total speakers, with about 278 million~\cite{eberhard2025ethnologue}. OCR supports the conversion of printed Bengali records into searchable text, but published evaluations report high recognition error rates. In the evaluation reported with the bbOCR system, Tesseract reaches an average character error rate (CER) of 0.78 across Bengali document types, and the purpose-built bbOCR system reaches 0.59~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}. A separate study puts Tesseract's character accuracy between 37 and 84 percent depending on the document type~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Several properties of the script complicate recognition. Consonants combine into conjunct glyphs, vowel signs attach before, after, above or below the consonant they modify, and many characters differ from their neighbours only in small strokes~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Historical books add worn type, uneven inking and ornate display lettering, and the corpora needed to train recognition models are small~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}.

Language-model post-correction offers a way to process recognition errors without modifying the OCR engine. Small models may suit institutions with limited computing resources, including national archives, government offices and non-governmental organisations involved in Bengali digitisation. Their lower hardware requirements motivate evaluating them for resource-limited archives~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. A correction layer of this kind would also be engine-independent: it reads text, not images, so it could be attached to any OCR system without retraining.

Evidence on whether such a layer works falls into three groups. Correction helped where the model was fine-tuned for the task~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,beshirov2025bulgarian} or where large models read nearly clean English text~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Zero-shot results were mixed across models and languages: open models improved English but not Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, and only one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models improved an English newspaper corpus~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. Correction made the text worse when the input was noisier or the language less resourced: zero-shot models, including a 70-billion-parameter one, raised the error rate once the input CER reached about 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, and zero-shot language models raised the word error rate of Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. To our knowledge, no published study evaluates language-model correction of Bengali OCR, compares one correction layer across OCR engines, or checks how much of a reported failure the generation settings themselves cause. We evaluate the following research questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can small open language models (0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters), prompted zero-shot, reduce the character and word error rates of OCR output for historical Bengali print?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Is the effect of correction consistent across OCR engines when the correction step is not adapted to either engine?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} What does correction do to the text: does it repair recognition errors, or does it change text that was already correct?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Do the decoding setting, the prompt, the chunking or a safeguard change the outcome?
\end{itemize}

No model significantly lowered the character error rate. The general-purpose models mostly copied their input, the Bengali-specific models regenerated it, and every method altered correct text. The study's contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item To our knowledge, the first evaluation of language-model post-correction for Bengali OCR: eight open models, two OCR engines and a dictionary baseline on 40 pages whose split was frozen before any model was run, with paired bootstrap intervals and Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests.
\item Evidence that a common decoding setting, a repetition penalty and n-gram ban that also act on the prompt, makes a copying model appear to damage the text, a pitfall for any evaluation of correction.
\item A clean-text control that separates over-correction from the difficulty of noisy input, and an analysis of what correction changes: correct words broken and wrong words fixed, edit operations, character classes, and copying versus regeneration.
\item Tests of remedies: three prompt variants, model-independent chunks, length and edit-distance safeguards, and correction restricted to words the OCR engine was unsure of.
\item Public code, page lists and prompts, so the study can be repeated on free computing resources.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

We review Bengali OCR systems, post-OCR correction before and with language models, Bengali language models and tokenisation, and the correction of Bengali and other Indic text.

\subsection{Bengali OCR systems}
Work on Bengali OCR has focused on recognition itself. bbOCR combines text detection, a purpose-built Bengali recognition model and layout reconstruction to convert whole documents into searchable text~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}; it is compared only against Tesseract, with average text-level CER of 0.59 against 0.78. Rabby et al.\ train separate word-segmentation models for computer-composed, letterpress, typewritten and handwritten documents, and report Tesseract accuracy of 37 to 84 percent against 83 to 90 percent for their system~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. At the level of single handwritten words, Safir et al.\ reach a CER of 0.091 and a WER of 0.273, using no surrounding context~\cite{safir2021endtoend}. For degraded printed Bangla, Chaudhury et al.\ separate text from background with a generative adversarial network and recognise it with an encoder-decoder model designed to reduce the word error rate~\cite{chaudhury2022deep}. These systems improve recognition itself but still leave many errors, especially on difficult and degraded pages, and none adds a separate correction step for them.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction before language models}
Post-OCR correction predates large language models; Nguyen et al.\ survey its approaches and note that OCR quality drops markedly on historical material~\cite{nguyen2021survey}. Two ICDAR competitions provided shared benchmarks: the 2017 edition used about 12 million characters of English and French monographs and periodicals from four centuries~\cite{chiron2017icdar}, and the 2019 edition about 22 million characters in ten European languages, where the best system improved the text by 44\%~\cite{rigaud2019icdar}. For low-resource settings, Rijhwani et al.\ corrected the OCR of three endangered languages with a model that can copy characters from the first-pass OCR, reducing the error rate by 34\% on average~\cite{rijhwani2020ocr}. For Bangla, Pal et al.\ corrected OCR errors by parsing words into roots and suffixes and looking each part up in a lexicon~\cite{pal2000ocr}. These methods learn from aligned OCR and reference text or rely on language-specific lexicons; we know of no comparable resource for historical Bengali print, which is one reason to test models that need no training.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction with language models}
Task-specific fine-tuning has improved OCR correction in prior studies. Llama 2 tuned on short segments of 19th-century English newspapers reduced CER by 54.5 percent, against 23.3 percent for a fine-tuned BART~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}. Results without such tuning are less consistent. Kanerva et al.\ prompted open-weight models of 8 to 70 billion parameters on English and Finnish; most improved the English text, but every open model made the Finnish text worse, and only GPT-4o improved it~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. Koynov and Doan found that zero-shot GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 70B cut CER by 48 to 58 percent on newspapers with an input CER of 0.07, but raised it by about 19 percent at 0.15 and, for Llama, by 41 percent on German text at 0.25~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Vershinin et al.\ ran four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models zero-shot on an English newspaper corpus (input CER 0.084); only one improved CER, and the others raised it, in one case five-fold~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. None of the instruction-tuned German models in another study reduced the average CER of a periodical, although the best one cut the word error rate by 22 percent; re-examining the outputs of an earlier study, the authors also found that inputs longer than about 400 characters degraded most~\cite{danilova2025german}.

The 2026 HIPE-OCRepair shared task compared zero-shot, fine-tuned and continued-pretraining systems of 8 to 24 billion parameters on English, French and German; the best system used continued pre-training and fine-tuning and finished ahead of the zero-shot systems, and over-correction of text that was already accurate was a recurring problem~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. In a study of Portuguese survey pages, a frontier model reduced CER from 11.7 to 4.7 percent~\cite{viana2026reframing}. Beshirov et al.\ improved historical Bulgarian, a non-Latin script, with a supervised two-stage system, but one that is specific to its engine and language~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}. Levchenko found that multimodal models transcribing 18th-century Russian text sometimes beat conventional OCR but inserted archaic characters from the wrong period~\cite{levchenko2025historical}. Do et al.\ report that spell correction performs poorly on historical documents, partly because modern and historical corpora differ, and anchor a language model to a reference text instead~\cite{do2025reference}. Across these studies, zero-shot correction helped mainly when large or frontier models read input with a CER below about 0.15, and over-correction of accurate text recurs.

\subsection{Bengali language models and tokenisation}
BanglaBERT is a Bengali encoder built for understanding tasks, not for rewriting text~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}. BanglaT5 is the corresponding sequence-to-sequence model for generation~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5}. TituLLMs are Bengali-pretrained Llama 3.2 models at 1B and 3B parameters, trained on about 37 billion tokens with an extended tokenizer~\cite{nahin2025titullms}. BanglaLlama continues the pretraining of other Llama models and reports that Bangla and Hindi together make up less than 0.21 percent of Llama 2's pretraining tokens, against 89.7 percent for English~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Mahfuz et al.\ study whether dedicated Bengali models are needed at all, and find that inefficient tokenisation of Bengali raises computational cost and lowers generation quality~\cite{mahfuz2025toolate}. Related tokenisation disparities are reported across languages. Petrov et al.\ show that English-centric tokenizers need several times more tokens for the same text in many languages, with Bengali among them (roughly five to ten times the English count), while multilingual tokenizers such as XLM-R and M2M100 stay at about 1.4 to 1.6 times~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer}. Ahia et al.\ also report higher costs and poorer language-model performance for over-segmented languages~\cite{ahia2023cost}. General multilingual models thus see little Bengali and tokenise it inefficiently, and Bengali-specific models have not, to our knowledge, been evaluated on OCR correction.

\subsection{Correction of Bengali and other Indic text}
The closest Bengali work corrects grammar, not OCR. Instruction-tuning raised Bangla grammar-correction scores by 3 to 7 points over the zero-shot setting, and humans still outperformed the models at correcting; the hardest categories for GPT-4o included mixing of the older literary register with the modern one~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. For Hindi speech-recognition output, fine-tuned models of 0.3 to 0.58 billion parameters beat much larger language models, and zero-shot GPT-4o-mini raised the word error rate from 28.6 to 31.8 and from 18.0 to 25.1 percent. The authors trace this to over-correction, which grows with model size~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Bangla spelling correction has supervised neural models: BSpell combines BERT with a convolutional sub-network and outperformed earlier checkers on two Bangla datasets and one Hindi dataset~\cite{rahman2023bspell}, and a transformer-based detector-purificator-corrector framework targets Bangla and other resource-scarce Indic languages~\cite{bijoy2025transformer}. Bengali correction work therefore exists for grammar and spelling in modern text, but not for OCR errors in historical print.

\subsection{Research gap}
Table~\ref{tab:literature} summarises the studies above. Among the work we reviewed, none evaluates post-OCR correction for Bengali, none applies open models below seven billion parameters to OCR correction or uses input with a CER above 0.25, and none compares one correction layer across OCR engines. These gaps motivate the choice of language, model scale and cross-engine evaluation.

\section{Methodology}

The evaluation compares raw and corrected OCR text against the same ground truth. The following subsections specify the dataset, OCR engines, correction configuration, models, comparison baseline and scoring protocol.

\subsection{Overview}
Figure~\ref{fig:method} shows the pipeline. Two OCR engines read each page image. Their raw text is split into chunks and passed to a correction model, and both the raw and the corrected text are scored against a manual transcription. OCR and correction are separate stages, so the same correction step can be attached to the output of any engine. No model is trained or fine-tuned, and every model is used as released.

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the ground-truth transcriptions released with the ICDAR 2019 Competition on Recognition of Early Indian Printed Documents (REID2019)~\cite{clausner2019reid}, produced by the British Library's Two Centuries of Indian Print project with the PRImA Research Lab and Jadavpur University~\cite{britishlibrary2019bengaligt}. The source volumes are Bengali books printed between 1713 and 1914. The digitised images are out of copyright and the transcriptions are in the public domain.

The release contains 81 page images with PAGE-XML annotations. Twenty-nine of them (36\%) have text regions marked but no transcribed text, so no error rate can be computed, and they are excluded; 52 pages remain. We drop the two pages with the thinnest transcriptions, measured by the share of text regions that actually contain text, which leaves a pool of 50 pages. The pool is split into 10 development pages and 40 evaluation pages, and the split was frozen in saved page-identifier lists and committed to version control before any correction model was run. The development pages were used only to debug the pipeline and as the source of the worked examples in one prompt variant; no development page contributes to any reported score. All 40 evaluation pages are scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). They come from 37 books, with at most two pages from any one book (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}); several are verse or title pages in ornate display type. A first version of this study scored a random sample of 15 of these pages (seed 403) on a CPU; those runs are used here only for the decoding comparison and for CPU timing.

\subsection{Baseline OCR}
Two open-source engines produce the noisy text. Tesseract~5.3.4~\cite{smith2007tesseract,tesseract5} runs with its LSTM recogniser and the Bengali \texttt{tessdata\_best} model, and EasyOCR~1.7.2~\cite{jaidedai2024easyocr} with Bengali enabled. Both use their default settings, which for Tesseract means automatic page segmentation (mode~3), and receive the page image as released (colour scans at 400 dpi, about 1{,}800 by 3{,}000 pixels), without preprocessing. Each engine produces one hypothesis per page. Two engines with different error profiles let us test whether correction behaves differently depending on which engine produced its input.

The engines read the whole page, while the ground truth covers only the annotated text regions, concatenated in the order of the PAGE-XML file. Text outside those regions, such as stamps, shelf marks or handwriting (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}b), therefore counts as inserted characters, and a different reading order counts as errors. On average the raw OCR is 1.03 (Tesseract) and 1.02 (EasyOCR) times the length of the reference (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}), so text outside the annotated regions is a small share of the output. This mismatch affects the raw and the corrected text in the same way, because the correction models receive the full raw OCR, so it raises absolute error rates but not the paired differences we test. A first version of this study ran the OCR in August 2026 with the Tesseract build then installed on Colab, whose version was not recorded; re-running it on the original 15 pages changed the Tesseract text on all 15 (mean CER 0.364 against 0.320 now) and left EasyOCR nearly unchanged. All 40 evaluation pages here use the October 2026 OCR.

\subsection{Correction layer}
\textbf{Zero-shot.} Here, zero-shot evaluation uses no task-specific weight updates, and the main prompt contains no worked examples. The decoder models receive an instruction and the OCR text; BanglaT5 receives the text alone, as detailed below. We chose this setting because we know of no large public set of Bengali OCR error pairs for training, and because it avoids the cost of task-specific training.

\textbf{Prompt.} The decoder models receive the same instruction, through each model's chat template:
\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
The following text was produced by OCR on a historical Bengali document and may contain recognition errors. Correct the OCR errors and output only the corrected Bengali text, with no explanation, preamble, or additional commentary.
\end{quote}
The OCR text follows the instruction. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and has no chat template, so it receives the OCR text alone and is run as a text-to-text model. This prompt was fixed in advance and never revised in response to model output.

\textbf{Prompt variants.} For the four general-purpose models we also test three variants, each changing one thing: the same instruction followed by ``Preserve the historical spelling of the original text; if you are not sure whether something is an error, leave it unchanged''; the instruction translated into Bengali; and the instruction with three worked examples. The examples are single lines from three different development pages, chosen by a fixed rule: the first reference line of 30 to 90 characters whose best-matching OCR line has a CER between 0.05 and 0.30 and contains no Latin letters, taking the engines in turn.

\textbf{Chunking.} Following Kanerva et al.~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, each page is divided into chunks of about 250 tokens of the model's own tokenizer before correction. Chunks are formed by grouping complete lines, so no chunk boundary falls inside a line. Because the tokenizers differ, the resulting chunks range from 168 characters on average for Llama 3.2 1B to 483 for BanglaT5 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). As a model-independent alternative we also group lines into chunks of at most 300 characters, which every model then receives identically. A page whose raw OCR is shorter than ten characters would be left unchanged, since the model would have almost nothing to work from; no evaluation page triggered this rule.

\textbf{Decoding.} Decoding is greedy, so output is deterministic. The maximum number of new tokens is $\min(512,\max(64,1.5n))$, where $n$ is the token length of the input chunk, and an output that reaches this limit is counted as truncated. No other constraint is applied. Our first runs also used a repetition penalty of 1.3 and banned repeated four-grams. We added these two settings after a development-set run in which one model repeated a single phrase until it reached the length limit, so, unlike the prompt, they were chosen after seeing model output. In the generation function of the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}, both settings act on the whole token sequence, which for a decoder-only model includes the prompt and therefore the OCR text being corrected. They penalise every token the model copies from its input and forbid it from reproducing any four-token run of that input. Because correction consists mostly of copying, we use the setting without them as the main condition and report the original setting as a decoding ablation (Section~\ref{sec:decoding}).

\subsection{Choice of models}
We evaluate eight models of 0.25 to 3.84 billion parameters (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Parameter counts are computed from each checkpoint's configuration, with tied input and output embeddings counted once.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma 2B~\cite{gemma2024} (2.51B parameters), Qwen3 1.7B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}, Phi-4-mini~\cite{abouelenin2025phi4mini} and Llama 3.2 1B~\cite{meta2024llama32} are open instruction-tuned multilingual models; Qwen3 and Phi-4-mini were released in 2025. Bengali is a very small share of the pretraining data of general models~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Qwen3 can produce a reasoning trace before its answer; we disable this through its chat template, so it answers directly.
\item \emph{Bengali-specific models.} TituLLMs 1B~\cite{nahin2025titullms} (checkpoint v1.1) is Llama 3.2 1B continually pretrained on Bengali text. It is a base model without instruction tuning, and it keeps the Llama vocabulary. We therefore also evaluate the instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints at 1B and 3B parameters (v2.0 Instruct), which use an extended vocabulary of 170{,}497 tokens. BanglaT5~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5} (about 248M parameters) is a Bengali sequence-to-sequence model, used as released without fine-tuning; it provides a sequence-to-sequence point of comparison.
\end{itemize}
Phi-4-mini replaces Phi-3 Mini, which was planned in the first version of this study but did not fit its CPU budget. BanglaBERT~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} was not considered because it is an encoder and cannot generate corrected text. All models are loaded with the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}.

\subsection{Classical baseline}
To test whether the outcome is specific to language models, we also correct the OCR with a dictionary spell-checker. SymSpell~\cite{garbe2025symspell} replaces every Bengali word that is not in its lexicon with the lexicon word at the smallest edit distance, breaking ties by frequency, if one exists within an edit distance of 2; known words, punctuation, digits and Latin text are left unchanged. The lexicon is the 50{,}000 most frequent words of the Bengali OpenSubtitles corpus~\cite{dave2018frequencywords}, of which 44{,}335 entries consist only of Bengali letters and signs. Its settings are the library defaults, fixed in advance.

\subsection{Computing setting}
The motivating setting is CPU-only use by institutions that process Bengali documents without GPU infrastructure. All the models are small enough to run on a CPU, and Table~\ref{tab:models} reports the CPU time per page for the five that were run on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in bfloat16, on the 15-page sample. To evaluate all 40 pages, eight models and the variants within a free compute budget, the reported runs use a free NVIDIA T4 GPU on Kaggle, in float32, or in float16 for the two models above 3B parameters, which do not fit the GPU's 15~GB in float32. On the pages where a CPU and a GPU run received identical input, the outputs of the general-purpose models were identical or nearly so, and the scores of the other models agreed (Section~\ref{sec:results}). No paid service is used at any stage.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\textbf{Normalisation and metrics.} Before scoring, reference and hypothesis are normalised to Unicode NFC and runs of whitespace are collapsed, because Bengali conjuncts and vowel signs can be encoded as different code-point sequences that look identical. Zero-width joiners and non-joiners are kept, and the danda counts as an ordinary character. CER and WER are computed with jiwer~\cite{vaessen2022jiwer} for each page and averaged over pages, so every page counts equally regardless of its length; we also report the pooled CER, the total number of character errors divided by the total reference length. CER counts code points, so an error in one sign of a conjunct counts once although the reader sees the whole cluster as wrong. We therefore also report a grapheme-cluster CER over extended grapheme clusters, which keep a conjunct and its vowel signs together as one unit. CER is unbounded: an output with more erroneous characters than the reference has characters scores above 1. We therefore also report the character match error rate, $\mathrm{cMER}=(S+D+I)/(H+S+D+I)$, which counts insertions in the denominator and is bounded in $[0,1]$~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Here $H$, $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the numbers of matched, substituted, deleted and inserted characters.

\textbf{Statistical testing.} For each engine and correction method, the unit of analysis is the per-page difference between the corrected and the uncorrected error rate. We report the mean difference with a 95\% percentile interval from a paired bootstrap of the 40 pages (10{,}000 resamples, seed 403). We also run a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on the same differences and adjust the $p$-values with the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979} over the 36 CER and WER tests (nine methods, two engines, two metrics), which follows the reporting of Viana~\cite{viana2026reframing}; the 18 cMER tests form a separate family. Because pages from the same book are not independent, we repeat the bootstrap resampling whole books instead of pages. The prompt and chunking variants are compared with the original setting in the same way, with their own Holm family.

\textbf{Validation.} The evaluation split was frozen before any model ran, and development pages are never scored. We used this one fixed evaluation set and did not perform cross-validation. No model was trained, and greedy decoding is deterministic, so runs were not repeated with different seeds. Each page is paired with its own uncorrected text.

\textbf{Clean-text control.} To separate over-correction from the difficulty of noisy input, each model also receives the reference text itself in place of the OCR, with the same prompt and decoding. Any change it makes can then only be damage.

\textbf{Word-level and edit analysis.} To see what correction changes, we align the ground-truth words with the raw OCR and with the corrected output. A ground-truth word that the raw OCR matched exactly and the output does not is a \emph{broken} word; a word the raw OCR missed and the output matches is a \emph{fixed} word~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. The \emph{change ratio} is the CER of the output measured against the raw OCR, divided by the CER of the raw OCR against the ground truth~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; a ratio above 1 means the model changed more than there was to repair. \emph{Invented runs} are runs of at least six characters that the output contains and the raw OCR does not~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. We also report output length relative to the reference, the share of Bengali, Latin and other characters, ground-truth word retention, edit operations against the reference, the error rate by character class, and the truncation rate.

\textbf{Safeguards.} As post-hoc checks, we replace a model's output by the raw OCR whenever its length falls outside 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length; these thresholds are taken from a zero-shot system in the HIPE-OCRepair task~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and are not tuned on our pages. A second rule keeps the output only if it differs from the raw OCR by a CER of at most $\tau$, for $\tau \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$, a grid fixed in advance.

\textbf{Confidence-gated correction.} Both engines report how confident they are: Tesseract per word (0 to 100) and EasyOCR per detected text box (0 to 1), which every word in the box inherits. We re-ran both engines with the same versions to obtain these values, which reproduced the stored OCR text on all 40 Tesseract pages and 37 EasyOCR pages; confidences are mapped onto the stored words by word alignment. The existing model outputs are then gated: after aligning the OCR words with the output words, an edit that replaces or deletes OCR words is kept only if every OCR word it touches has a confidence below a threshold, and an inserted word only if a neighbouring OCR word does; all other words stay as the OCR read them. The model therefore still corrects whole chunks with their context, but may change only what the engine was unsure of. The thresholds are fixed in advance: 50, 70 and 90 for Tesseract and 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9 for EasyOCR.

\textbf{Deviation from the intended protocol.} Kanerva et al.\ strip leading and trailing text that does not align with the input, such as explanatory preambles~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. We specified this step but did not implement it, so all scores use unfiltered model output and any preamble or commentary counts as error. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what this means.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The page lists, prompts, few-shot selection rule, and the OCR, correction, baseline, scoring and statistics code are public~\cite{rahman2026repo}, with the frozen split committed before any model was run. Table~\ref{tab:models} names each checkpoint and the revision used. The GPU runs (10--12 October 2026) used Transformers 5.19.0, the OCR used EasyOCR 1.7.2 and Tesseract 5.3.4, and scoring used jiwer 4.0.0 under Python 3.11; other packages were those of the free Kaggle and Colab images on the run dates. Nothing is trained and no paid service is used, so the study can be repeated end to end on free computing resources.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results compare each corrected output with the raw OCR of the same page. We first report the main comparison, then the clean-text control, the decoding, prompt and chunking variants, the agreement between engines and across pages, and the robustness of the statistics.

\subsection{Baseline OCR performance}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} gives the error rates of the raw OCR text before any correction. EasyOCR is better than Tesseract on all three metrics (CER 0.297 against 0.341). This agrees with the high Tesseract error rates on Bengali reported in earlier work~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr,rabby2024enhancement}. We did not find a published comparison of the two engines on historical Bengali print. Both baselines are high in absolute terms: EasyOCR, the better engine, has a WER of 0.757.

\subsection{Effect of correction}
Table~\ref{tab:matrix} gives the full evaluation matrix and Fig.~\ref{fig:delta} shows the change in CER with its interval. No method lowered the character error rate significantly for either engine; restricting edits to words the OCR engine was unsure of gives one small exception (Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}). Of the 36 CER and WER comparisons, 23 are significant after Holm correction, and 22 of them are increases.

The methods fall into two groups. Gemma 2B, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini stay close to the raw OCR: their CER changes lie between $-0.004$ and $+0.017$, and none is significant. Llama 3.2 1B raises CER slightly but significantly, from 0.341 to 0.380 on Tesseract output and from 0.297 to 0.330 on EasyOCR output ($p=0.002$ and $p=0.017$). The dictionary baseline raises CER by 0.037 and 0.040 and WER by 0.091 and 0.095 (all $p<0.001$). The four Bengali-specific models raise CER by between 0.52 and 1.34: BanglaT5 to 0.860 and 0.831, TituLLMs 1B to 1.084 and 1.033, TituLLMs 3B Instruct to 0.958 and 0.886, and TituLLMs 1B Instruct to 1.642 and 1.633. The instruction-tuned 1B checkpoint is therefore worse than the base checkpoint it was meant to improve on.

The one significant improvement is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, whose WER falls from 0.827 to 0.799 ($-0.028$, 95\% CI $[-0.047,-0.013]$, $p=0.002$). Its grapheme-cluster CER also falls, from 0.431 to 0.417 ($-0.015$, $[-0.032,-0.004]$, $p=0.021$ after Holm correction over 18 tests), but its code-point CER does not change significantly ($-0.004$). Qwen3 thus repairs some whole words on Tesseract text without lowering the overall character error rate; on EasyOCR text it shows no improvement on any metric.

\subsection{Clean-text control}
\label{sec:clean}
Table~\ref{tab:clean} shows what each method does when it receives the reference text itself. Every method changes correct text. Gemma 2B and Qwen3 1.7B change the least, with a CER of 0.032 and 0.035 against the reference, and they leave 6 and 2 of the 40 pages exactly unchanged. Phi-4-mini reaches 0.060, the dictionary baseline 0.057 (it replaces about 31 correct words per page, WER 0.268) and Llama 3.2 1B 0.119. The Bengali-specific models produce CERs between 0.83 and 1.57 on text that contained no error. Because these inputs have nothing to repair, the changes are pure over-correction, and its size ranks the methods in nearly the same order as the main comparison.

\subsection{Effect of the decoding setting}
\label{sec:decoding}
Table~\ref{tab:decoding} compares the original decoding setting, with the repetition penalty and the four-gram ban, against plain greedy decoding. Both settings exist for the four models of the first version on its 15-page sample, run on a CPU. For the three decoder-only models, removing the two constraints lowered CER significantly on both engines: by 0.22 for Gemma 2B, by 0.41 and 0.56 for Llama 3.2 1B, and by 1.07 and 1.56 for TituLLMs. Under the original setting Gemma 2B raised CER from 0.364 to 0.583 and from 0.297 to 0.517; under plain decoding it returns its input almost unchanged, with a median CER of 0.012 and 0.005 between its output and that input. Most of the error increase reported in the first version is therefore attributable to the decoding constraints, not to the models' corrections. The constraints also changed the kind of failure: under them, 34\% of TituLLMs' output characters were Latin, mostly English commentary on the text, against 1\% with plain decoding. BanglaT5, an encoder-decoder model whose decoder does not see the input tokens as its own history, changed by only 0.011 and 0.020.

\subsection{Prompt and chunking variants}
Table~\ref{tab:variants} reports the four general-purpose models under the three prompt variants and the model-independent chunks. No variant brings any model significantly below the raw OCR. The preserve-spelling instruction lowered mean CER in seven of the eight model-and-engine pairs, by up to 0.026 (Llama 3.2 1B on EasyOCR output), but none of these changes is significant. The Bengali prompt and the three worked examples raised CER in most pairs; the increase is significant for Llama 3.2 1B with the Bengali prompt on both engines and with the examples on Tesseract output, and for Phi-4-mini with the Bengali prompt on EasyOCR output. Chunks of at most 300 characters changed mean CER by at most 0.016, with no significant change.

\subsection{The engine-agnostic question}
The effect of correction agrees closely between the two engines. The mean CER changes differ by at most 0.035 between Tesseract and EasyOCR output for every method. The same three models (Gemma 2B, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini) stay closest to the raw OCR on both engines, and the four Bengali-specific models are the four most harmful on both, in the same order. The only difference in direction is Qwen3 1.7B, whose CER change is $-0.004$ on Tesseract output and $+0.017$ on EasyOCR output, both close to zero. These are descriptive comparisons; they do not establish equivalence in effect size.

\subsection{Per-page analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} plots the change in CER on each page against the page's baseline CER. Two patterns appear. For the general-purpose models and the dictionary baseline, corrected CER follows baseline CER almost exactly (Spearman $\rho$ between 0.88 and 1.00 on both engines): these methods leave most of each page as it was, so the page's own difficulty determines its score. Qwen3 1.7B lowered CER on 22 of the 40 Tesseract pages and Gemma 2B on 16, but by amounts that their increases on other pages offset. For BanglaT5 and the TituLLMs models, corrected CER is unrelated or only weakly related to baseline CER ($\rho$ between $-0.00$ and 0.55): their output has a similar error rate whatever the input quality, which suggests that they regenerate the text instead of editing it. For BanglaT5 the falling line in Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} follows directly from this, since its corrected CER stays near 0.85 on every page.

When the pages are split into thirds by baseline CER, BanglaT5, TituLLMs 1B and TituLLMs 3B Instruct have their smallest increase on the hardest third for both engines; for TituLLMs 3B Instruct on Tesseract output, for example, the increase is $+0.85$ on the easiest third and $+0.39$ on the hardest. The dictionary baseline shows the same pattern on a smaller scale ($+0.047$ against $+0.021$ on Tesseract output). The changes of the general-purpose models stay within 0.07 of zero in every third. The HIPE-OCRepair task also reports over-correction of low-noise input~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}.

\subsection{Robustness of the statistics}
The conclusions do not depend on how the error is measured or how pages are resampled. The pooled CER is lower than the per-page mean (0.298 against 0.341 for raw Tesseract output, 0.286 against 0.297 for EasyOCR), because short pages have higher error rates, but it ranks the methods in the same way, and on it no method is below the raw OCR (Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output: 0.299 against 0.298). Grapheme-cluster CER is higher than code-point CER (0.431 and 0.387 for the raw OCR), because one wrong sign makes a whole conjunct wrong; on this metric only Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output improves, as reported above. On the bounded cMER (Table~\ref{tab:damage}) the baselines are 0.290 and 0.260, and 13 of the 18 tests are significant after Holm correction, all increases; they include Llama 3.2 1B on both engines and Phi-4-mini on EasyOCR output (0.279, $p=0.010$), whose CER changes were smaller. Resampling whole books instead of pages, and grouping pages more coarsely by collection, gives practically the same intervals, as expected with 37 books for 40 pages.

\subsection{CPU and GPU runs}
On the five pages whose EasyOCR text was byte-identical between the CPU and the GPU runs, Gemma 2B produced the same output on all five, and Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B outputs differed from their CPU counterparts by a CER of at most 0.004. The outputs of TituLLMs and BanglaT5 differed more (CER 0.20 and 0.44 between runs), but their scores agreed (0.945 against 0.954 and 0.806 against 0.813). The change of hardware alters individual degenerate outputs, not the conclusions.

\section{Failure Analysis}

To characterise what correction changed, we examine word retention, edit operations, character classes and truncation. We then evaluate whether the safeguards, including confidence-gated correction, reduce the error increases.

\subsection{Word retention and word-level changes}
Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics} reports how many ground-truth words each output retains. Raw Tesseract output keeps 46\% of the distinct ground-truth words and raw EasyOCR output 53\%. Pooled over both engines, the general-purpose models keep 46\% to 49\%, the dictionary baseline 37\%, and the Bengali-specific models between 3\% and 16\%. The word-level counts in Table~\ref{tab:damage} separate correct words that were changed from wrong words that were fixed. Gemma 2B broke 4\% of the words the raw OCR had right and fixed 0.4\% and 0.5\% of those it had wrong. Qwen3 1.7B broke 3\% and 5\% and fixed 3.3\% and 3.5\%, the closest balance of any method. Phi-4-mini and Llama 3.2 1B broke 8\% to 11\%, the dictionary baseline 29\% and 31\%, and the Bengali-specific models between 74\% and 99\%. The change ratio is below 1 for the general-purpose models and the dictionary (0.1 to 0.4) and between 3.9 and 7.0 for the Bengali-specific models.

\subsection{Edit profiles by model}
The methods differ in which edit operations increase (Table~\ref{tab:editops}; counts per page against the reference).

\emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma 2B, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini leave substitutions, deletions and insertions close to the raw OCR's counts (on Tesseract text, Gemma 2B: 93, 76 and 64 against 92, 60 and 69). Llama 3.2 1B mainly deletes: deletions rise from 60 to 110 per page on Tesseract text and from 58 to 93 on EasyOCR text.

\emph{Dictionary baseline.} SymSpell adds substitutions (92 to 111 and 84 to 108), because it replaces historical spellings that are missing from its modern lexicon with modern words.

\emph{BanglaT5.} Its output is on average 0.46 times the reference length. Deletions rise to 503 and 434 per page and insertions fall to near zero. About a third of its non-space output characters are neither Bengali nor Latin letters, that is, punctuation, ASCII digits and other symbols. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and was not fine-tuned for correction, which may account for this, but we did not test it.

\emph{TituLLMs.} The three checkpoints fail in different ways. The base 1B model substitutes and inserts (370 substitutions and 384 insertions per page on Tesseract text), reached the generation limit on every page, and repeats itself: in 51 of its 80 outputs a segment of at least 20 characters, split at danda, line breaks and vertical bars, recurs three or more times. The 1B Instruct checkpoint produces output 2.10 times the reference length, with 797 and 722 insertions per page; 33\% and 30\% of its output consists of runs of at least six characters absent from the input, it reached the generation limit on 80\% and 92\% of the pages, and it repeats itself in 34 of 80 outputs. The 3B Instruct checkpoint returned an empty page in 27 of its 80 outputs, and in 37 it wrote a Bengali description of the text (for example, that the text appears to be a copy of a historical document) instead of a corrected version; its deletions rise to 432 and 458 per page.

\emph{Character classes.} The raw OCR is least accurate on Bengali digits (65\% and 53\% of reference digits substituted or deleted), on the danda (59\% for Tesseract; EasyOCR never outputs a danda) and on punctuation, and most accurate on consonants (19\% and 14\%). Characters inside conjuncts are wrong in 29\% and 20\% of cases. The general-purpose models raise the error rate of every class slightly; the largest change is Gemma 2B on digits (65\% to 75\% and 53\% to 73\%). We did not label the pages by type (verse, title, prose), so we cannot break the errors down by page type.

\subsection{Truncation}
TituLLMs 1B reached the generation limit on every page and TituLLMs 1B Instruct on 80\% and 92\%. The other methods reached it on at most 25\% of pages (BanglaT5 on EasyOCR text), and Gemma 2B never did. BanglaT5 and TituLLMs 3B Instruct have high error rates with little truncation, so a high error rate does not require truncation.

\subsection{Effect of the safeguards}
\label{sec:safeguards}
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:damage} applies the length safeguard. It replaces outputs only for the Bengali-specific models: BanglaT5 on 21 and 8 of the 40 pages, TituLLMs 3B Instruct on 18 and 23, and TituLLMs 1B Instruct on 5 and 6. This lowers their CER, for example from 0.958 to 0.660 for TituLLMs 3B Instruct on Tesseract output, but every one of them remains significantly worse than the raw OCR after Holm correction. TituLLMs 1B never triggers the safeguard, because its outputs stay within 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length, and the general-purpose models never trigger it either. The edit-distance cap, which keeps an output only if it differs from the raw OCR by a CER of at most $\tau$, does not bring any method meaningfully below the raw OCR at any $\tau$ in the grid; the closest is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output at 0.337 to 0.339 against 0.341. For the Bengali-specific models the cap rejects almost every page, so their capped output is essentially the raw OCR.

Confidence gating (Table~\ref{tab:gated}) works at the word level instead of the page level. The engines are unsure of much of the text: 41\% of Tesseract words have a confidence below 50 and 77\% below 90, and 52\% of EasyOCR words lie in boxes below 0.5. Gating removes most of the damage of the harmful methods, for example lowering the CER of TituLLMs 1B on Tesseract output from 1.084 to 0.454 and that of the dictionary baseline from 0.378 to 0.348 at the lowest threshold, but nearly all of them remain significantly worse than the raw OCR. For the general-purpose models gating keeps the CER at or just below the raw OCR on Tesseract output. Qwen3 1.7B at threshold 70 gives the only significant reduction of CER in this study, from 0.341 to 0.337 ($-0.004$, 95\% CI $[-0.006,-0.002]$, $p=0.038$ after Holm correction over 54 tests), about 1\% of the baseline. On EasyOCR output, where confidence is per box and therefore coarse, no gated method improves on the raw OCR. Lower thresholds, which allow fewer changes, give the lowest error throughout.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main practical question is whether a correction step offers an advantage over keeping the raw OCR. The results also raise the question of why the models change text that the OCR had read correctly, and why the first version of this study overstated that harm.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\textbf{RQ1.} No method lowered the character error rate significantly for either engine, on the per-page CER or the bounded cMER, and on the pooled CER no method is below the raw OCR. The best general-purpose models, Gemma 2B, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini, leave the error rate essentially where it was. The only significant gains are by Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, which lowered WER by 0.028 and grapheme-cluster CER by 0.015, and, when its edits were restricted to words the engine was unsure of, CER by 0.004, about 1\%.

\textbf{RQ2.} The effect agrees across the two engines. The CER changes differ by at most 0.035 between Tesseract and EasyOCR output, and the methods fall into the same groups on both. This supports consistency in direction and in the ranking of methods across the two tested engines, not equivalence in magnitude.

\textbf{RQ3.} Correction does little repair. The general-purpose models mostly return their input: their corrected CER follows the baseline page by page, and they fix a few percent of the wrong words while breaking a similar or larger share of the right ones. The Bengali-specific models regenerate the text instead of editing it, break most correct words, and reach similar error rates whatever the input quality. The clean-text control shows that every method also alters text that contains no error at all.

\textbf{RQ4.} Of the settings we varied, only decoding changed the conclusion. On the 15-page sample, the repetition penalty and four-gram ban of the first version turned Gemma 2B's CER change on Tesseract output from none into an increase of 0.22, and raised the increase of Llama 3.2 1B from 0.07 to 0.48 and that of TituLLMs from 0.57 to 1.63. The preserve-spelling instruction reduced the damage slightly, the Bengali prompt and worked examples increased it, model-independent chunks made no difference, and the length and edit-distance safeguards brought no method below the raw OCR. Confidence gating removed most of the damage of the harmful methods but lowered CER below the raw OCR only once, by 0.004.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
The results are consistent with several prior reports of unsuccessful zero-shot correction. Open models degraded Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, zero-shot models degraded text once the input CER reached 0.15, even at 70 billion parameters~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, zero-shot models degraded Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}, and the HIPE-OCRepair task found over-correction of accurate input~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Correction worked where it was fine-tuned for the task~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}, where a frontier model read moderately noisy text~\cite{viana2026reframing}, or where the input was clean English~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Our setting combines factors that those studies report separately: models of 0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters, no task-specific training, a low-resource non-Latin script, historical spelling, and an input CER of 0.30 to 0.34, above the 0.25 or less of the other studies we reviewed. The supervised Bulgarian system improved correction on a non-Latin script~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}. Its language- and engine-specific training differs from the zero-shot, text-only approach evaluated here. Our decoding comparison adds a methodological point to this literature: with the generation settings we first used, a model that mostly copies its input appears to damage it, so reported failures of zero-shot correction should be checked against plain decoding.

\subsection{Possible explanations}
Correction requires preserving most of the input while repairing recognition errors. The experiments test some explanations for why the models fail at this and leave others open.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Input noise is not the main cause.} The first version of this study suggested that the input was too noisy for correction. The clean-text control contradicts this: every method changed text that contained no error, and the Bengali-specific models reached CERs of 0.83 to 1.57 on it. The difficulty analysis points the same way: for three of the four Bengali-specific models the increase is smallest on the hardest pages and largest on the easiest.
\item \emph{Tokenisation does not explain the ranking.} English-centric tokenizers need several times as many tokens as English for the same Bengali content~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer,ahia2023cost}. Across our models the number of tokens per character varies fourfold, from 0.29 to 1.22, but it does not predict the outcome: the TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints have among the lowest values and the highest error rates, while Llama 3.2 1B and TituLLMs 1B share the same tokenizer and the highest value, yet one stays close to the baseline and the other triples its error rate.
\item \emph{Historical spelling is a plausible factor, not a tested one.} Our sources use older forms of Bengali, which a model or lexicon built on the modern language may treat as errors. The dictionary baseline, whose lexicon is modern, replaced about 31 correct words per page of clean text, and the preserve-spelling instruction slightly reduced the damage in seven of the eight model-and-engine pairs of general-purpose models. Mixing of the older and the modern literary register is also among the categories that GPT-4o handled worst in grammar correction~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. We did not test this factor directly.
\item \emph{Instruction-following is a plausible factor for the Bengali-specific models.} The instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints often described the text, explained it, or returned nothing, instead of returning a corrected version, and the 1B Instruct checkpoint did worse than the base model it was built from. This behaviour suggests that their instruction tuning did not cover a copy-and-edit task like this one, but we did not assess instruction-following separately.
\end{itemize}
These results concern the evaluated checkpoints and configuration. They do not show that other Bengali-specific models, or the same models after fine-tuning, would fail.

\subsection{Practical implications}
For this historical corpus, keeping the raw OCR was at least as good as any correction we tested, and it avoids about two to fourteen minutes of CPU time per page (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The best general-purpose models are close to harmless but give no meaningful benefit in CER, and the Bengali-specific models and the dictionary spell-checker make the text worse. If correction is applied at all, restricting it to words the OCR engine was unsure of keeps the general-purpose models harmless, but the gain is about 1\% at best. For work that uses the Transformers generation function on copy-heavy tasks such as correction, the repetition penalty and n-gram ban should be avoided or limited to the generated tokens, because by default they also act on the prompt. Results from other languages motivate evaluating fine-tuned small models~\cite{kumar2026postasr,thomas2024leveraging}; the present experiment does not evaluate that alternative.

\subsection{Threats to validity and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Internal validity.} The decoding constraints of the first version were chosen after seeing development output; we therefore report both settings. The main runs used a GPU in float32 or float16, while the motivating setting is a CPU; on identical inputs the general-purpose models produced identical or nearly identical outputs on both. Tesseract output changed between the August and October 2026 runs, so all 40 pages use the October OCR. The few-shot examples come from development pages, which were also used for debugging.
\item \emph{No output filter.} The alignment step that strips preambles was not implemented, so methods that add commentary are penalised for it, the TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints most. Gemma 2B, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini add almost none and still do not improve CER, so commentary does not explain the absence of improvement.
\item \emph{Construct validity.} The engines read the whole page while the ground truth covers the annotated regions, so text outside them counts as error; this raises the absolute error rates of raw and corrected text alike. CER weights every code point equally; grapheme-cluster CER, WER and cMER give the same conclusions except for the small gain of Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output.
\item \emph{Statistical validity.} With 40 pages from 37 books the intervals are moderately wide, and small effects such as Llama 3.2 1B's became significant only with 40 pages. Holm correction controls the family-wise error within each family of tests, but not across the families reported in this paper.
\item \emph{External validity.} The pages are early printed books, 1713 to 1914, from one release, so the results may not transfer to modern printed Bengali or to other collections. We tested zero-shot use only, models up to 3.8 billion parameters, two open OCR engines, and four prompts; larger models, fine-tuning, a stronger OCR engine and a breakdown by page type were outside the scope of this study. EasyOCR reports confidence per text box, so gating on its output is coarse.
\item \emph{Scope of the analysis.} Section~V describes how the outputs differ from the input and the reference. It does not apply attribution or other model-internal explanation methods, so the untested explanations in this section are hypotheses.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

On 40 pages of historical Bengali print, none of eight small language models, prompted zero-shot, significantly lowered the character error rate of Tesseract or EasyOCR output, and neither did a dictionary spell-checker. Restricting their edits to words the OCR engine was unsure of removed most of the damage, but lowered the error rate by at most about 1\%. The best general-purpose models mostly returned their input, so their output was about as accurate as the raw OCR; one of them, Qwen3 1.7B, slightly lowered the word error rate on Tesseract output. The Bengali-specific models regenerated, described or abandoned the text and raised the error rate substantially. Every method altered text that contained no error. A common anti-repetition setting, which also acts on the prompt, had made copying models appear harmful in our first runs, and removing it was the only change that altered the conclusion; prompt wording, worked examples, chunking and the safeguards did not. Future work should test task-specific fine-tuning, including byte-level and Bengali sequence-to-sequence models, and larger models.
