\begin{abstract}
Small open language models could offer a low-cost correction step for optical character recognition (OCR) of historical Bengali books, but their value needs to be measured against human transcriptions. We tested four general-purpose models, four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints and a spell-checker on the output of two OCR engines, without worked examples. We first used 40 pages from one collection of books printed between 1713 and 1914, then tested the same settings once on 41 held-out pages from books that share no group with any page used before. We also trained two byte-level correctors for the task, ByT5-small and BanglaByT5, choosing their checkpoints on separate validation pages. On the held-out pages no method lowered the character error rate (CER): it rose significantly in 17 of the 18 zero-shot tests and, for both trained correctors, on Tesseract text. Two zero-shot models and both trained correctors lowered the word error rate in some settings, but their other edits added at least as many character errors as their repairs removed. Every method changed text that was already correct. On the 40 pages, repetition constraints intended to stop looping made the outputs much worse. % GATING-PENDING: one sentence on gating on the held-out pages
For comparable material, raw OCR should be retained until a correction step has been validated against reference transcriptions.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Bengali OCR, post-OCR correction, small language models, zero-shot prompting, historical documents, low-resource NLP, negative results

\section{Introduction}

Archives, libraries and research collections that hold scanned Bengali print need faithful machine-readable text to search their collections, extract information from them, or supply passages to document-based AI systems. OCR turns page images into text but leaves recognition errors, and historical Bengali print is a hard case. Tesseract's character accuracy ranges from 37 to 84 percent across Bengali document types~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}; small stroke differences distinguish many characters, conjunct glyphs combine consonants, and vowel signs can sit before, after, above or below their consonant~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Worn type, uneven inking and ornate display lettering add to the difficulty of historical books.

A text-only correction step can be used with an existing OCR engine, and small open models may suit institutions with limited computing resources~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. The difficulty is preserving a transcription while correcting it. A fluent rewrite may replace correct words or historical spellings, so a reduction in recognition errors is useful only if it outweighs the new errors. We therefore measure repair and preservation separately.

Previous evaluations give mixed results. Fine-tuned models have improved OCR text~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,beshirov2025bulgarian}, and large models have helped on nearly clean English~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Without task-specific training, open models helped English but not Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}; one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models improved an English newspaper corpus~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}; and a 70-billion-parameter model raised error rates at an input CER of about 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Bengali OCR correction has relied on lexicons, morphology and edit distance~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}. A commercial model has also cleaned OCR text from modern textbooks, but that step was not evaluated against reference transcriptions~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. We found no evaluation of language-model correction on historical Bengali print. Decoding settings introduce another concern. The Transformers library offers a repetition penalty and a ban on repeated $n$-grams to stop looping, but both also count the prompt~\cite{hf2026logits}. For correction, this means penalising the model for copying text it may need to preserve.

We evaluate one correction step applied to the output of two OCR engines, with a control in which the error-free reference is the input. The study has two parts. A 40-page evaluation, with many exploratory analyses on the same pages, explores how the models behave. A held-out test on 41 pages from other books then checks the main result once, with every setting fixed in advance and reading order and text regions reviewed by hand. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Under the evaluated conditions, do open language models, prompted without task-specific examples, improve historical Bengali OCR relative to keeping the raw output, as measured by character and word error rates?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} How much correctly recognised text do they preserve, and how many recognition errors do they repair?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} How do decoding constraints and the tested safeguards change correction quality?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Does a fully trained, task-specific corrector, chosen on separate validation pages, do better than the zero-shot models?
\end{itemize}
We use region-level OCR and synthetic noise to test whether input quality explains the results. Larger models, a briefly fine-tuned pilot and two methods without a language model provide further comparisons. Table~\ref{tab:experiments} identifies the models and pages in each experiment. The study contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled evaluation of zero-shot post-correction of historical Bengali OCR: eight open models and a dictionary baseline, read by two engines, on 40 pages of one collection and then, unchanged, on 41 book-disjoint held-out pages with reviewed reading order; character- and word-level outcomes are reported separately, and the 40-page evaluation is extended to region-level OCR, synthetic noise and larger models.
\item Two fully trained byte-level correctors, ByT5-small and BanglaByT5, trained on aligned OCR pairs and synthetic errors, selected on validation pages and tested once on the held-out pages, reported apart from the unadapted checkpoints.
\item A preservation analysis: a clean-text control, counts of correct words broken and wrong words fixed, and an edit-level account of why the word-level repairs of some models do not lower CER.
\item A decoding ablation showing that repetition constraints substantially worsen copying models, on OCR text and on error-free input; in the Transformers library they also act on the prompt, and restricting them to generated tokens removes much, but not all, of the damage. Tests of prompt variants, confidence gating and engine fusion complete it.
\end{enumerate}
The code, page lists and prompts are public, and the per-page OCR text and model outputs will be released with the paper (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Bengali OCR systems}
Bengali OCR research has mostly addressed recognition itself, and document type matters: Rabby et al.\ train separate word-segmentation models for computer-composed, letterpress, typewritten and handwritten material, obtaining character accuracy of 83 to 90 percent compared with Tesseract's 37 to 84 percent~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Other work operates at different scales. Safir et al.\ recognise single handwritten words without surrounding context, with CER 0.091 and WER 0.273~\cite{safir2021endtoend}. Chaudhury et al.\ address degraded printed Bangla by separating text from background with a generative adversarial network, then using an encoder-decoder recogniser designed to reduce word error rate~\cite{chaudhury2022deep}. These approaches improve recognition but leave errors on difficult or degraded pages. None supplies a separate correction step for those errors.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction before language models}
Nguyen et al.'s survey describes post-OCR methods developed before large language models and notes the poorer recognition quality of historical material~\cite{nguyen2021survey}. Shared benchmarks came from the ICDAR competitions: about 12 million characters of English and French monographs and periodicals spanning four centuries in 2017~\cite{chiron2017icdar}, then about 22 million characters in ten European languages in 2019, where the best system improved the text by 44\%~\cite{rigaud2019icdar}. Copying is useful in low-resource correction. A model that can copy characters from first-pass OCR reduced error rates by an average of 34\% across three endangered languages~\cite{rijhwani2020ocr}. Bengali approaches have relied on lexical resources. Pal et al.\ parse words into roots and suffixes for lexicon lookup~\cite{pal2000ocr}; Ahmed et al.\ use edit distance and character n-grams with about 541{,}000 dictionary words, lowering error rates by about 3\% on 50 documents~\cite{ahmed2016improving}. Such methods require aligned OCR/reference pairs or language-specific lexicons. We know of no comparable resource for historical Bengali print, hence our interest in models that require no task-specific training.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction with language models}
Table~\ref{tab:literature} compares the studies closest to ours. We selected them through targeted searches up to October 2026, including a semantic search of published papers; this is not a systematic review. Task-specific training accounts for several positive results: fine-tuned Llama 2 and BART lowered CER on 19th-century English newspapers~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}, the best HIPE-OCRepair systems combined continued pre-training with fine-tuning and outperformed the zero-shot ones~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}, and fine-tuning on synthetic character-level corruption outperformed training on real OCR pairs~\cite{bourne2025scrambled}. Task-specific instruction tuning of German models also lowered WER on historical periodicals, without reducing average CER~\cite{danilova2025german}. Results without such training are less consistent, even for large models. Fourteen foundation models were ``anything but efficient'' at correcting historical transcripts~\cite{boros2024postcorrection}, and one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models helped an English newspaper corpus~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}.

\emph{Input quality} matters too: zero-shot GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 70B lowered newspaper CER at an input CER of 0.07 but raised it at 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, a frontier model helped Portuguese survey pages at 0.12~\cite{viana2026reframing}, and over-correction of text that was already accurate recurs, in HIPE-OCRepair and for GPT models on historical English~\cite{zhang2024prosody}.

\emph{Language and register} matter as well: open models improved English but not Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, a supervised system improved historical Bulgarian but was specific to its language and engine~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}, modern spelling correction suits historical text poorly~\cite{do2025reference}, and multimodal transcription of 18th-century Russian introduced characters from the wrong period~\cite{levchenko2025historical}. Among the studies surveyed here, zero-shot correction helped mainly with large or frontier models and input CER below about 0.15.

\subsection{Bengali language models and tokenisation}
Bengali models differ in whether they can generate text at all. BanglaBERT is an encoder for understanding tasks and cannot rewrite OCR output~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}; BanglaT5 is a sequence-to-sequence generation model~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5}. The TituLLMs family uses Bengali-pretrained Llama 3.2 models at 1B and 3B, trained on about 37 billion tokens with an extended tokenizer~\cite{nahin2025titullms}. BanglaLlama continues pretraining other Llama models. Its authors report that Bengali and Hindi together account for less than 0.21 percent of Llama 2's pretraining tokens, compared with 89.7 percent for English~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Limited exposure is only part of the concern. Mahfuz et al.\ find that inefficient Bengali tokenisation increases computation and reduces generation quality when assessing the need for dedicated Bengali models~\cite{mahfuz2025toolate}. Across languages, Petrov et al.\ show that English-centric tokenizers require several times more tokens for equivalent content; Bengali needs roughly five to ten times the English count, compared with about 1.4 to 1.6 times for multilingual XLM-R and M2M100 tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer}. Ahia et al.\ also associate over-segmentation with higher cost and poorer model performance~\cite{ahia2023cost}. General multilingual models therefore face both limited Bengali exposure and inefficient tokenisation. BanglaByT5 addresses both concerns with a byte-level encoder-decoder pretrained on 14 GB of Bengali literary and newspaper text~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglabyt5}. IndicBART is another compact sequence-to-sequence backbone, pretrained for 11 Indic languages and English~\cite{dabre2022indicbart}. We train BanglaByT5 as one of two task-specific correctors (Section~\ref{sec:trained}); IndicBART is not evaluated here. To our knowledge, Bengali-pretrained models have not been evaluated on OCR correction.

\subsection{Correction of Bengali and other Indic text}
The closest Bengali language-model correction study concerns grammar rather than OCR. Instruction-tuning improved its scores by 3 to 7 points over zero-shot use, though humans still did better. Mixing the older literary register with the modern register was among GPT-4o's hardest error categories~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. In Hindi speech-recognition correction, fine-tuned models of 0.3 to 0.58 billion parameters outperformed much larger models. Zero-shot GPT-4o-mini increased WER from 28.6 to 31.8 and from 18.0 to 25.1 percent; the authors attribute this to over-correction that grows with model size~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. For Indic OCR, fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence models improved Sanskrit text, the byte-level ByT5 most~\cite{maheshwari2022sanskrit}, and a fine-tuned model given the surrounding sentence improved Hindi, Gujarati and Marathi OCR~\cite{bhandari2026contextual}. Supervised Bengali spelling systems include BSpell, which combines BERT and a convolutional sub-network and outperforms earlier checkers on two Bengali datasets and one Hindi dataset~\cite{rahman2023bspell}, and a transformer detector-purificator-corrector for Bengali and other resource-scarce Indic languages~\cite{bijoy2025transformer}. BnMMLU provides an example of language-model use after Bengali OCR: GPT-3.5 copy-edited EasyOCR output from modern textbooks and exam guides during dataset preparation. The authors report fewer formatting and spelling errors and manually check about 10\% of the items, but give no reference-based error rate for that step~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}.

\subsection{Research gap}
The Bengali studies cover modern grammar and spelling, lexicon-based OCR correction~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}, or unmeasured cleaning of modern print; we found no evaluation of language-model correction on historical Bengali print. Among the post-OCR studies in Table~\ref{tab:literature}, none prompts open models below seven billion parameters, applies one correction step across OCR engines, or tests how decoding settings change the result, and the highest input CER any of them reports is 0.254~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, below that of our whole-page OCR (0.30 to 0.34). We address these gaps with eight core models of 0.25B to 3.84B parameters, first on 40 pages and then on a held-out test set, and add two fully trained correctors. Exploratory extensions on the 40 pages examine additional checkpoints up to 32B parameters and a briefly fine-tuned pilot.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Overview}
Each page passes through two OCR engines, then through a correction model in chunks (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). We score both versions of the text against its manual transcription. Keeping recognition and correction separate lets the same correction step accept output from any engine. The zero-shot models are used as released. Two evaluations use them. The 40-page evaluation covers eight core models and a dictionary baseline; diagnostic analyses and extensions on the same pages cover the subsets listed in Table~\ref{tab:experiments} (Section~\ref{sec:further}). Because these pages were reused for many analyses, and 8 of them influenced the region-cropping settings, we treat the 40-page results as exploratory. The held-out test (Section~\ref{sec:testset}) then scores the same nine methods once on 41 pages from other books, together with two correctors trained for the task (Section~\ref{sec:trained}); its results carry the confirmatory claims. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, which were not trained to follow correction instructions, and the briefly fine-tuned pilot are reported as diagnostic tests of unadapted or undertrained models, apart from the trained correctors.

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the ground-truth transcriptions released with the ICDAR 2019 Competition on Recognition of Early Indian Printed Documents (REID2019)~\cite{clausner2019reid}, produced by the British Library's Two Centuries of Indian Print project with the PRImA Research Lab and Jadavpur University. We use version~2 of the British Library's release, whose XML coordinates were improved~\cite{britishlibrary2019bengaligt}. The source volumes are Bengali books printed between 1713 and 1914. The digitised images are out of copyright and the transcriptions are released under the Public Domain Mark.

The release contains 81 page images with PAGE-XML annotations. The British Library also catalogues a 155-image collection comprising the 81 REID2019 images and 74 images used for Transkribus training~\cite{britishlibraryBengaliCollection}. The 40-page evaluation uses the updated REID2019 release; the Transkribus images, checked for book overlap, supply the held-out test (Section~\ref{sec:testset}). Of the 81 REID2019 pages, twenty-nine (36\%) have text regions marked but no transcribed text, so no error rate can be computed, and they are excluded; 52 pages remain. We drop the two pages with the thinnest transcriptions, measured by the share of text regions that actually contain text, which leaves a pool of 50 pages. The pool is split into 10 development pages and 40 evaluation pages, and the split was frozen in saved page-identifier lists and committed to version control before any correction model was run. The development pages were used to debug the pipeline, as the source of the worked examples in one prompt variant, to estimate the synthetic noise model, as historical training lines for the fine-tuned model, and to choose its checkpoint; they do not contribute pages to a reported evaluation-set estimate. The split is by page, not strictly by book: one book identifier is shared between development and evaluation, so the fine-tuning pilot is not a book-held-out experiment. All 40 evaluation pages are scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). They come from 37 books, with at most two pages from any one book (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The release gives no publication date per book. Each page was labelled by its main content, from its transcription, by a single annotator without a second check: 10 title pages, often in ornate display type, 8 verse pages (including verse followed by a commentary), 13 prose pages, and 9 lists (tables of contents, indexes, tables and dictionary entries); the labels are released with the code. A random sample of 15 of the evaluation pages (seed 403) was also processed on a CPU; it provides the CPU timings and an earlier decoding comparison.

\subsection{Held-out test set}
\label{sec:testset}
The 40 evaluation pages were reused for many analyses, so we built a second, book-disjoint test set and evaluated it once. Its pages come from the 74 Transkribus images of the British Library collection~\cite{britishlibraryBengaliCollection}, whose transcriptions are stored as ALTO XML. On 20 November 2026, before any model saw these pages, we pooled them with the REID2019 pages and froze a new split (Table~\ref{tab:testsplit}). Books were merged into groups, by known aliases of the same shelfmark and by siblings, shelfmarks that are equal once their last part is dropped (for example 279\_23\_D\_6, D\_7 and D\_8), and every group went to one side of the split only. The test set takes every Transkribus page whose group contains no REID2019 page, if it has at least 300 reference characters and at least half of them Bengali: 41 pages from 36 book groups, with 45{,}080 reference characters. The other pages form a training set of 62 pages and a validation set of 14, drawn as whole groups with a fixed seed; they serve only the trained correctors of Section~\ref{sec:trained}. One Transkribus page was excluded because it duplicates a REID2019 evaluation page byte for byte. A check of the page lists confirms that no test page shares a book with the training, validation, development or 40 evaluation pages, even one shelfmark level up.

\textbf{Text regions and reading order.} The ALTO files declare no reading order, and some Transkribus line boxes lie outside their text block. To keep reference mismatches from counting as recognition errors, the author reviewed every test page against its image before any score was computed, and the text itself was never edited. Blocks follow the page as it is read: the title first, then each column from top to bottom, and the lines of a row from left to right. Running headers and section letters stay where they sit, and dot leaders count as text. Six non-text or empty boxes were removed, ten boxes were redrawn, and 37 boxes were added for printed lines whose ALTO line lay outside its block. Nineteen pages needed a different block order. This gives 1{,}250 scoring units, 1{,}213 kept blocks and 37 added boxes. Seven pages are tables of contents, which hold many short blocks of titles, dot leaders and page numbers; they stay in the test set and are also reported separately, a choice made on layout before any score.

\textbf{OCR and scoring.} Each unit is cropped from the page (its reviewed box, widened to include its own word boxes, plus 8 pixels) and read by Tesseract 5.3.4 (page segmentation mode~6, \texttt{tessdata\_best}) and EasyOCR 1.7.2 on a Kaggle CPU. On the 1{,}167 units whose crop did not change in the review, this run reproduced the text of an earlier run exactly, for both engines. Every method corrects each unit on its own; a unit longer than about 250 tokens is split into line chunks as in the 40-page evaluation, and units under 10 characters are passed through unchanged. The main metric is the per-block CER of a page: the edit distances of its units are summed and divided by the sum of their reference characters, and pages are then averaged. Because each unit is compared with its own reference, reading order between units cannot add errors. We did not average per-unit CERs, because the many one- to three-character units of the contents pages would dominate such a mean. WER is computed in the same way over words. The settings, the metric and the statistical tests below were fixed before any test score existed, and each test was scored once.

\subsection{Baseline OCR}
\label{sec:ocr}
Two open-source engines produce the noisy text. Tesseract~5.3.4~\cite{smith2007tesseract,tesseract5} runs with its LSTM recogniser and the Bengali \texttt{tessdata\_best} model, and EasyOCR~1.7.2~\cite{jaidedai2024easyocr} with Bengali enabled. Both use their default settings, which for Tesseract means automatic page segmentation (mode~3), and receive the page image as released (colour scans at 400 dpi, about 1{,}800 by 3{,}000 pixels), without preprocessing. Both ran on a free Google Colab CPU runtime on 11 October 2026. Each engine produces one hypothesis per page. Two engines with different error profiles let us test whether correction behaves differently depending on which engine produced its input.

The engines read the whole page, while the ground truth covers only the annotated text regions. Text outside those regions, such as stamps, shelf marks or handwriting (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}b), therefore counts as inserted characters. The reference joins the region transcriptions in the order in which they appear in the PAGE-XML file; 30 of the 40 files also declare a reading order, and on 17 pages it differs from the file order. The OCR text keeps each engine's own reading order and is not matched to the reference, so a different order also counts as errors. The mismatch need not cancel in the paired differences, since a model may treat out-of-region text differently from the rest. We therefore repeat the main comparison against a reference in the declared reading order, and with a word-recall measure that ignores word order and extra text (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}), and we repeat the whole evaluation with each region read separately (Section~\ref{sec:further}). OCR output is not stable across installations: an earlier run of Tesseract on the 15-page sample, with an unrecorded Colab build from August 2026, gave different text on all 15 pages (mean CER 0.364 against 0.320), while EasyOCR changed little. Tesseract also drifted within one version. On 12 October, a rerun on Colab with the same version and model file reproduced the main run's text on all 40 pages (this rerun supplied the confidence scores, Section~\ref{sec:gatemethod}). On 17 October, the same setting on Colab and a conda-forge build on Kaggle gave text identical to each other but different from the main run (CER 0.22 between them). The Bengali model file, image decoding and resolution were ruled out; the Leptonica version, image libraries and CPU instruction sets of these runs were not recorded, so the cause cannot be identified. The OCR scripts now record them, with the full version report of Tesseract and a hash of the model file. All reported whole-page results therefore use one stored OCR run, which will be released with the paper (Section~\ref{sec:repro}); only the CPU timings and the earlier decoding comparison on the 15-page sample use the August text.

\subsection{Correction layer}
\label{sec:correction}
\textbf{Zero-shot.} Each model is used as released, without training on correction examples, and the main prompt contains no worked examples. The decoder models receive an instruction and the OCR text; BanglaT5 receives the text alone, as detailed below. We chose this setting because we know of no large public set of Bengali OCR error pairs for training, and because it avoids the cost of task-specific training.

\textbf{Prompt.} The decoder models receive the same instruction, through each model's chat template:
\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
The following text was produced by OCR on a historical Bengali document and may contain recognition errors. Correct the OCR errors and output only the corrected Bengali text, with no explanation, preamble, or additional commentary.
\end{quote}
The OCR text follows the instruction. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and has no chat template, so it receives the OCR text alone and is run as a text-to-text model. This prompt was fixed in advance and never revised in response to model output.

\textbf{Prompt variants.} For the four general-purpose models we also test three variants, each changing one thing: the same instruction followed by ``Preserve the historical spelling of the original text; if you are not sure whether something is an error, leave it unchanged''; the instruction translated into Bengali; and the instruction with three worked examples. The examples are single lines from three different development pages, chosen by a fixed rule: the first reference line of 30 to 90 characters whose best-matching OCR line has a CER between 0.05 and 0.30 and contains no Latin letters, taking the engines in turn.

\textbf{Chunking.} Following Kanerva et al.~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, each page is divided into chunks of about 250 tokens of the model's own tokenizer before correction. Chunks are formed by grouping complete lines, so no chunk boundary falls inside a line. Because the tokenizers differ, the resulting chunks range from 168 characters on average for Llama 3.2 1B to 483 for BanglaT5 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). As a model-independent alternative for the four general-purpose models, we also group lines into chunks of at most 300 characters, which every model then receives identically; a single longer line would form its own chunk, but none occurred on our pages.

\textbf{Decoding.} We use greedy decoding, without token sampling. The maximum number of new tokens is $\min(512,\max(64,\lfloor1.5n\rfloor))$, where $n$ is the token length of the input chunk, and an output that reaches this limit is counted as truncated. No other constraint is applied in the main condition. A repetition penalty and a ban on repeated $n$-grams are the library's remedies when greedy decoding loops. Both are off unless set (the library's neutral values are a penalty of 1.0 and no ban); a user who meets looping output, as we did on some development pages, may add them. We evaluate them, alone and together, as a decoding ablation (penalty 1.3; repeated four-grams banned; both together are \emph{guarded} decoding; Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). In the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}, \texttt{generate} applies \texttt{RepetitionPenaltyLogitsProcessor} and \texttt{NoRepeatNGramLogitsProcessor} to the whole token sequence, which for a decoder-only model includes the prompt and so the OCR text being corrected~\cite{hf2026logits}. In version~5.19.0, which the runs used, the penalty can exclude the prompt only if its processor is constructed by hand, and the $n$-gram ban has no such option. A one-step test with Qwen3 0.6B on that version confirms both: the penalty changed the scores of all 42 distinct token ids of a correction prompt and of no other id, and the ban blocked the token that would repeat a four-token run of the prompt. The constraints then penalise every token the model copies and forbid it to reproduce any four-token run of its input. A follow-up run restricts each constraint to the generated tokens, the penalty through \texttt{prompt\_ignore\_length} and the ban through a processor that sees only the output, for the four general-purpose models on Tesseract and clean text. Since correction is mostly copying, plain greedy decoding is the main condition; decoding settings that suit one generation task need not suit another~\cite{wiher2022decoding}.

\subsection{Choice of models}
We evaluate eight core models of 0.25 to 3.84 billion parameters (Table~\ref{tab:models}). We call a model \emph{small} if it has at most about four billion parameters, so that it can run on a CPU; the larger models of Section~\ref{sec:further} are scale extensions. Parameter counts are computed from each checkpoint's configuration, with tied input and output embeddings counted once.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma-2B-it~\cite{gemma2024card} (Gemma~1, 2.51B parameters), Qwen3 1.7B~\cite{qwen2025card}, Phi-4-mini~\cite{phi4mini2025card} and Llama 3.2 1B~\cite{meta2024llama32} are open instruction-tuned multilingual models; Qwen3 and Phi-4-mini were released in 2025. Bengali is a very small share of the pretraining data of general models~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Qwen3 can produce a reasoning trace before its answer; we disable this through its chat template, so it answers directly.
\item \emph{Bengali-pretrained checkpoints.} TituLLMs 1B~\cite{nahin2025titullms} (checkpoint v1.1) is Llama 3.2 1B continually pretrained on Bengali text. It is a base model without instruction tuning and keeps the Llama vocabulary; the template shipped with its tokenizer only concatenates the messages, so it received our instruction and the OCR text as plain text to continue, with no cue for where a corrected version should begin. As a check, we also gave it a completion prompt without template, which states that the corrected text follows the OCR text and ends with ``Corrected text:'' (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). We also evaluate the instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints at 1B and 3B parameters (v2.0 Instruct), which use an extended vocabulary of 170{,}497 tokens; their model cards give no prompt format, and their shipped template wraps the prompt in an Alpaca-style instruction and response frame, which we used; as a check, they also received the completion prompt on Tesseract text. BanglaT5~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5} (about 248M parameters) is a sequence-to-sequence model pretrained only to restore corrupted text spans and released without task fine-tuning. Without a task prefix it acts as a denoiser rather than a corrector, so we report it as a control. Its model card also asks for inputs passed through the authors' text normaliser, which the main runs did not apply; a rerun with it is reported in Section~\ref{sec:precision}.
\end{itemize}
These results therefore describe checkpoint-and-prompt configurations, not a ranking of model families, and say nothing about Bengali pretraining in general; we treat the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints as diagnostic cases. BanglaBERT~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} was not considered because it is an encoder and cannot generate corrected text. All models are loaded with the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}. Section~\ref{sec:further} adds newer and larger general-purpose models, up to 32 billion parameters, and one fine-tuned model.

\subsection{Classical baseline}
To test whether the outcome is specific to language models, we also correct the OCR with a dictionary spell-checker. SymSpell~\cite{garbe2025symspell} replaces every Bengali word that is not in its lexicon with the lexicon word at the smallest edit distance, breaking ties by frequency, if one exists within an edit distance of 2; known words, punctuation, digits and Latin text are left unchanged. The lexicon is the 50{,}000 most frequent words of the Bengali OpenSubtitles corpus~\cite{dave2018frequencywords}, of which 44{,}335 entries consist only of Bengali letters and signs. Its settings are the library defaults, fixed in advance. Because subtitles are modern and colloquial, we also test a lexicon of the 100{,}000 most frequent words of Bengali Wikipedia~\cite{wikimedia2023wikipedia} (143{,}069 articles), and an edit distance of 1 with each lexicon.

All language-model scores of the 40-page evaluation, its extensions and the held-out test, however, come from GPU runs; only the CPU timings and the earlier decoding comparison on the 15-page sample come from CPU runs of language models, and the dictionary baseline and engine fusion also ran on a CPU. Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the CPU time per page for the five core models that were run on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in bfloat16, with plain decoding, on the 15-page sample and its August OCR text, so these times describe a different input from the main runs. \subsection{Metrics}
Before scoring, reference and hypothesis are normalised to Unicode NFC and runs of whitespace are collapsed, because Bengali conjuncts and vowel signs can be encoded as different code-point sequences that look identical. Zero-width joiners and non-joiners are kept, and the danda counts as an ordinary character. The character error rate is $\mathrm{CER}=(S+D+I)/N$, where $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the numbers of substituted, deleted and inserted characters in the alignment of the hypothesis with the reference and $N$ is the number of reference characters; the word error rate (WER) is the same quantity over words. Words are whitespace-delimited tokens after normalisation; punctuation is retained, so a word with an attached danda differs from the same word without it. This tokenisation also applies to wMER, word recall and word-level alignments. Both CER and WER are computed with jiwer~\cite{vaessen2022jiwer} for each page and averaged over pages, so every page counts equally regardless of its length; the pooled CER divides the total number of character errors by the total reference length. CER counts code points, so an error in one sign of a conjunct counts once although the reader sees the whole cluster as wrong; the grapheme-cluster CER is computed over extended grapheme clusters, which keep a conjunct and its vowel signs together. CER and WER are unbounded, since an output with many insertions can score above 1. We therefore also report the character and word match error rates, $\mathrm{cMER}=(S+D+I)/(H+S+D+I)$ and its word-level counterpart wMER, where $H$ is the number of matched units; both are bounded in $[0,1]$~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Three further normalisations test the scoring choices: mapping ASCII digits to Bengali digits, removing zero-width joiners and non-joiners, and writing khanda-ta as the single code point U+09CE. CER and WER are the primary measures; CER always means the code-point CER unless named otherwise. The grapheme-cluster CER, cMER, wMER, pooled CER, a CER with all spaces removed and the reference-word recall of Section~\ref{sec:analyses} are secondary checks. They are not interchangeable: recall ignores word order and extra text, so it cannot penalise insertions or a wrong order, and it does not reward a repair that leaves a word wrong.

\subsection{Statistics}
For each engine and correction method, the unit of analysis is the per-page difference between the corrected and the uncorrected error rate. We report the mean difference with a 95\% percentile interval from a paired bootstrap of the 40 pages (10{,}000 resamples, seed 403), and the number of pages whose error rate fell or rose. We also run a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on the same differences. Pages whose difference is exactly zero, which are common when a method leaves the OCR unchanged, are dropped before ranking, the default of the test, so we report how many pages remain. On the 40 pages, the main family is the 36 CER and WER tests (nine methods, two engines, two metrics), adjusted with the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979}, which follows the reporting of Viana~\cite{viana2026reframing}. Every other test in this paper is exploratory: each table that reports one names its own Holm family, and Holm controls the error only within a family. Because pages from the same book are not independent, we repeat the bootstrap resampling whole books instead of pages. The four general-purpose models stay close to the raw OCR, but no detectable change is not the same as no change. We therefore test equivalence with two one-sided paired $t$-tests (TOST) against margins of $\pm0.01$ and $\pm0.02$ CER. The margins are about 3\% and 6\% of the raw CER, a change a user comparing transcriptions would hardly notice, and small next to the changes of the other methods. Because per-page differences can be heavy-tailed, we also check whether the 90\% paired-bootstrap interval lies within the margin; it gives the same decisions. Table~\ref{tab:experiments} lists every test family with its size and role: the confirmatory held-out family described below, exploratory families with Holm correction within each, and descriptive analyses without tests.

The evaluation split was frozen before any model ran, and development pages never contribute to a reported evaluation-set estimate. We used this one fixed evaluation set and did not perform cross-validation. Greedy decoding removes sampling randomness, so the zero-shot runs were not repeated with different seeds; it does not guarantee identical outputs across devices or software builds (appendix). The fine-tuning pilot was trained once, with one seed (403).

\textbf{Held-out test.} The same paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon test are used over the 41 test pages, on the per-block CER and WER. Three Holm families were fixed before any test score: the nine zero-shot methods (36 tests: two metrics, two engines), which is the confirmatory family of the study; the two trained correctors (8 tests); and confidence gating (54 tests, exploratory, as on the 40 pages). Because the 40 pages were reused for many analyses, their 36-test family, called primary in the first version of this study, is now treated as exploratory as well.

\subsection{Analyses}
\label{sec:analyses}
\textbf{Sensitivity to the reference.} Two checks address the mismatch between whole-page OCR and region-level ground truth. First, we rebuild each reference with its regions in the reading order declared in the PAGE-XML file, where there is one (regions it does not list follow in file order), and repeat the paired comparison of CER. Second, we compute reference-word recall: the share of reference word tokens that also occur in the output, counted as a multiset, $\sum_w\min(c_{\mathrm{ref}}(w),c_{\mathrm{out}}(w))/\sum_w c_{\mathrm{ref}}(w)$, where $c(w)$ is the token count of word $w$. It ignores word order and does not penalise extra words, so neither a different reading order nor OCR text from outside the annotated regions lowers it; it does not reward repairs that leave a word wrong.

\textbf{Clean-text control.} To separate over-correction from the difficulty of noisy input, each model also receives the reference text itself in place of the OCR, with the same prompt and decoding. Any change it makes can then only be damage. The control is a diagnostic stress test of preservation under the tested prompt configuration; it does not estimate the quality of corrected OCR, and it does not rank the models in general.

\textbf{Word-level and edit analysis.} We align the ground-truth words with the raw OCR and with the corrected output. A ground-truth word that the raw OCR matched exactly and the output does not is a \emph{broken} word; a word the raw OCR missed and the output matches is a \emph{fixed} word~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. The \emph{change ratio} is the CER of the output measured against the raw OCR, divided by the CER of the raw OCR against the ground truth~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; a ratio above 1 means the model changed more than there was to repair. \emph{Invented runs} are runs of at least six characters that the output contains and the raw OCR does not~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. To see why repaired words need not lower CER, we assign every character error of the alignment with the reference to a reference word (an insertion to the preceding word when its preceding reference character belongs to a word, otherwise to the word at the insertion point; remaining boundary insertions are counted separately) and split the change in character errors between fixed, broken and still-wrong words and the spaces between words. Word correctness comes from the two word alignments; character errors come from independent global character alignments and are grouped by reference-word index, so their totals reconcile even when word boundaries change. An insertion before the first character belongs to the first word. Each error is also labelled by the Bengali character class involved (consonant, vowel sign, virama, other sign, digit, punctuation or space). Output length, script shares, word retention, edit operations, character classes, page types and truncation are reported in the appendix.

\textbf{Commentary filter.} As a check on prompt fit, a generous filter drops every output line of which fewer than 30\% of the character trigrams occur in the page's OCR input. It removes preambles and descriptions of the text, but also invented lines and occasionally correct ones, so it is a rough diagnostic of how much of the error commentary accounts for, not a bound on what a filter could achieve and not one of the evaluated methods. EasyOCR detector segments produce shorter output lines than Tesseract; the trigram rule can therefore respond differently to edits in the two engines' outputs.

\textbf{Attribution.} To see which part of its input each output token is sensitive to, we adapt Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} to token positions: the score is the logit of each generated token, and the map runs over the token positions of a layer's hidden states. We compute the literal version, which averages the gradient over positions, and an element-wise version, which multiplies gradient and hidden state at each position before summing over the hidden dimensions, at one-half and three-quarter depth, leaving out the first token, special tokens and the predicting position. For each generated token we record the share of the map on the OCR text, the rest of the prompt and the model's own earlier output, and, for a copied token, whether the map peaks within one position of its source. We scored up to 24 generated tokens in one chunk from each of 10 pages (seed 403), on Tesseract and clean text, for the eight core models.

\textbf{Output filter not applied.} Kanerva et al.\ strip leading and trailing text that does not align with the input, such as explanatory preambles~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. We did not apply this step, so all main scores use unfiltered model output and any preamble or commentary counts as error; the commentary filter above shows how much this matters.

For reproducibility, let $h_{i,k}$ be hidden dimension $k$ at position $i$ and $g_{i,k}=\partial y_t/\partial h_{i,k}$ for the target token logit. The attribution scores are
\begin{align}
\alpha_k &= T^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{T}g_{i,k},\\
A_i^{\mathrm{literal}} &= \max(0,\sum_k\alpha_k h_{i,k}),\\
A_i^{\mathrm{element}} &= \max(0,\sum_k g_{i,k}h_{i,k}).
\end{align}
After excluded positions are set to zero, each map is divided by its sum; a zero-sum map stays zero. Depth indices use Python's nearest-integer rounding (ties to even), clipped to $[1,L]$. Copied tokens are identical token-ID runs aligned with \texttt{SequenceMatcher}, with automatic junk detection off. The reported chance rate averages $\min(3,L_{\mathrm{OCR}})/L_{\mathrm{OCR}}$ across scored copied tokens; it approximates a uniform random peak in the OCR positions and does not correct the three-position window at its boundaries.

\subsection{Safeguards and confidence gating}
\label{sec:gatemethod}
\textbf{Page-level safeguards.} As post-hoc checks, we replace a model's output by the raw OCR whenever its length falls outside 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length; these thresholds are taken from a zero-shot system in the HIPE-OCRepair task~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and are not tuned on our pages. A second rule, an edit-distance cap, keeps the output only if its CER measured against the raw OCR is at most $\tau$, for $\tau \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$, a grid fixed in advance. Each safeguarded result is compared with the raw OCR by the same paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon test, with Holm correction over the 18 length-rule tests and, separately, the 72 edit-cap tests.

\textbf{Confidence gating.} Both engines report how confident they are: Tesseract per word (0 to 100) and EasyOCR per detected text box (0 to 1), which every word in the box inherits. We re-ran both engines with the same versions on Colab on 12 October 2026, one day after the main run and before the drift (Section~\ref{sec:ocr}), to obtain these values; the re-run reproduced the stored OCR text on all 40 Tesseract pages and 37 EasyOCR pages, and confidences are mapped onto the stored words by word alignment. On the three EasyOCR pages that were not reproduced, as elsewhere, a stored word without a counterpart in the re-run counts as confident, so the gate never lets a model change it. The existing model outputs are then gated: after aligning the OCR words with the output words, an edit that replaces or deletes OCR words is kept only if every OCR word it touches has a confidence below a threshold, and an inserted word only if at least one adjacent OCR word does (at a boundary, the sole available neighbour). We align whitespace tokens with \texttt{SequenceMatcher} (automatic junk detection off); a multi-word replacement is accepted only if every OCR word in that alignment block is below the threshold; all other words stay as the OCR read them. The model therefore still corrects whole chunks with their context, but may change only what the engine was unsure of. The thresholds are fixed in advance: 50, 70 and 90 for Tesseract and 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9 for EasyOCR.

\subsection{Further experiments}
\label{sec:further}
Five further experiments test explanations of the main result: noisy or mismatched input, model size, the lack of training, and the use of a language model at all. Unless stated otherwise they use plain greedy decoding, the main prompt and the main chunking, and each forms its own exploratory Holm family.

\textbf{Region-level OCR.} Each PAGE-XML text region is cropped from the page image (its bounding box plus 8 pixels) and read on its own, Tesseract with page segmentation mode~6, which suits a single block of text. The crop settings were chosen in a comparison on 8 of the evaluation pages with a different Tesseract version (5.5.3), before any model saw region text. These pages therefore influenced the choice, and the region-level OCR scores are not fully independent of it; each correction is still compared with the raw OCR of the same regions. The 576 regions with text are joined in reading order and scored against the reference regions in the same order, so out-of-region text and reading-order differences no longer count as errors. Because of the Tesseract drift (Section~\ref{sec:ocr}), whole pages were read again in the same run, and region-level Tesseract output is compared only with that run; EasyOCR reproduced the main run (CER 0.002 between them). All eight models, the dictionary and Qwen3 4B correct the region text.

\textbf{Synthetic noise.} A character-level noise model, estimated from the OCR of the 10 development pages only (substitution, deletion and insertion rates per character, both engines pooled), corrupts the 40 references. One scale factor per level, found by bisection, sets the mean CER to 0.020, 0.048, 0.100, 0.199 and 0.297. The four general-purpose models correct each level, which locates any input CER at which correction starts to help.

\textbf{Newer and larger models.} Qwen3 0.6B, 4B and 8B~\cite{qwen2025card}, Gemma~3 1B and 4B~\cite{gemma3card2025} (instruction-tuned; the 4B model receives text only) and Llama 3.2 3B Instruct~\cite{meta2024llama32} correct all 40 pages of both engines and the clean text, in float16 above 3 billion parameters and otherwise in float32, except Gemma~3 4B, which overflows in float16 and runs in float32 on two GPUs. Qwen3 32B runs with 4-bit NF4 weights~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} and float16 computation on two T4 GPUs. Because of its cost it corrects only the 10 pages of the attribution analysis, and every other model is rescored on the same 10 pages for comparison.

\textbf{Fine-tuning.} ByT5-small~\cite{xue2022byt5}, a sequence-to-sequence model of about 300 million parameters whose tokens are bytes, so that Bengali needs no special tokenizer, is fine-tuned to turn corrupted text into clean text. ByT5 was the strongest corrector of Sanskrit OCR~\cite{maheshwari2022sanskrit}, and synthetic corruption has replaced aligned OCR data in English~\cite{bourne2025scrambled}, which historical Bengali lacks. The training text is about 40{,}000 Bengali Wikipedia segments of 60 to 300 characters plus the reference lines of the 10 development pages, repeated 20 times to add historical spelling, with any segment containing a contiguous 25-character substring of an evaluation reference removed after training-text preprocessing and whitespace collapse. This is a substring exclusion, not a book-level split. One pair in ten is left clean, so the model also learns to copy; the others are corrupted with the development-page noise model at a CER drawn uniformly from 0.02 to 0.40. Training uses AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, effective batch 8) on one T4 GPU for 150 minutes, a budget that counts the development evaluations; the last evaluation, started within the budget, ended at 155 minutes. Training keeps the checkpoint with the lowest CER on the real OCR of the development pages. At test time, chunks of at most 300 characters of whole lines are corrected with their lines joined by spaces, as in training, and greedy decoding. A first run passed the line breaks on to the model, which had never seen one in training; it skipped and repeated whole lines, so we fixed the input format and report the second run.

\textbf{Engine fusion.} A method without a language model combines the two engines word by word. EasyOCR, the engine with the lower CER, is the base text, aligned word by word with Tesseract's. Where the two disagree over at most three words on each side, Tesseract's reading replaces EasyOCR's if its confidence ranks higher within the page; the ranks put the two engines' confidence scales on one footing. Within each page and engine, finite confidences receive their average zero-based rank divided by $n-1$; missing confidences, or fewer than two finite values, receive rank 1. A block uses the mean rank of its words. Replacement requires a strictly higher mean rank; ties keep EasyOCR. A second rule requires the mean rank to exceed EasyOCR's by more than 0.25. Longer disagreements, and words only one engine read, keep EasyOCR. The confidences are those of the confidence gating (Section~\ref{sec:gatemethod}). Fusion is compared with EasyOCR only, its base; it needs two OCR readings, so it has no clean-text control.

\subsection{Fully trained correctors}
\label{sec:trained}
The zero-shot models were not trained for correction, and the fine-tuning pilot saw only part of its data once. To test a corrector built for the task, we trained two byte-level sequence-to-sequence models in full: ByT5-small~\cite{xue2022byt5}, and BanglaByT5~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglabyt5}, which continues ByT5's pretraining on Bengali text. Bytes need no Bengali tokenizer, and ByT5 was the strongest corrector of Sanskrit OCR~\cite{maheshwari2022sanskrit}. Both were trained on the same data from the training pages of Table~\ref{tab:testsplit}, read by the same engine builds in one run. For the real pairs, every text block of the 62 training pages, read by both engines, was aligned with its reference and cut into pieces of at most 300 reference characters. Pieces with a length ratio outside 1/3 to 3 or an OCR CER above 0.6 were dropped as failed crops. This left 1{,}185 pairs, each repeated ten times. For the synthetic pairs, a character noise model was fitted on the training pages' OCR only. It corrupted 60{,}062 Bengali Wikipedia segments and the 976 reference lines of the training pages (repeated ten times) at a random CER between 0.02 and 0.40, and one pair in ten was left clean so that the model also learns to copy. This gave 69{,}822 pairs. Any piece that shared a 25-character string with a validation or test reference was removed. Training used batches of 8 pairs, a learning rate of $3\times10^{-4}$ with 500 warm-up steps, and inputs of up to 1{,}024 bytes, on two Kaggle T4 GPUs. Every 500 updates the model corrected the 14 validation pages block by block, the checkpoint with the lowest mean page CER was kept, and training stopped after five evaluations without improvement. It stopped after 4{,}500 updates for ByT5-small (best at 2{,}000) and 6{,}500 for BanglaByT5 (best at 4{,}000), less than one pass over the 81{,}672 pairs, because validation CER had stopped improving while training loss still fell. The test pages were then corrected and scored once. The validation outputs showed both models writing runs of a danda-like mark in place of digits, so we also report a guarded variant that falls back to the raw OCR of a block when the output hits the length cap, is less than half or more than twice the input length, or contains a run of six identical characters that the input lacks. This rule was written after reading the validation outputs; it is secondary, and the plain output is the result.

\label{sec:results}

Each comparison pairs a corrected page with its raw OCR; a change $\Delta$ is corrected minus raw, so positive values mean the correction made the text worse. Neither gives clean input for correction: even EasyOCR has WER 0.757.

The held-out test checks the main result once, on 41 pages from books the study had not used (Section~\ref{sec:testset}). On these pages the raw OCR has a per-block CER of 0.350 for Tesseract and 0.272 for EasyOCR, and a per-block WER of 0.742 and 0.744.

The rest of this section reports the 40-page evaluation and its extensions. These results are exploratory (Section~\ref{sec:testset}); where they overlap with the held-out test, the held-out result takes precedence.

\subsubsection{40-page evaluation}
\label{sec:main}
These whole-page scores also count text outside the annotated regions and differences in reading order as errors (Section~\ref{sec:ocr}). Both affect raw and corrected text, but a model may treat them differently, so the region-level evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:region}), which removes both at the source, isolates the correction step most cleanly; its raw and corrected text come from one OCR run per engine. We report both, and they agree. The main, ungated methods do not significantly reduce CER for either engine (Table~\ref{tab:matrix} and Fig.~\ref{fig:delta}). Confidence gating gives one small exception, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:gating}. After Holm correction, 23 of the 36 CER/WER comparisons are significant; 22 indicate increased error.

The methods fall into two groups. Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini stay close to the raw OCR: their CER changes lie between $-0.004$ and $+0.017$, none significant. Not significant is not the same as unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}): equivalence within $\pm0.02$ CER holds only for Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract and Gemma-2B-it on EasyOCR output, and within $\pm0.01$ for none; Phi-4-mini raises the bounded wMER significantly on both engines; and each model makes some pages better and others worse (Gemma-2B-it 16 and 16 of the 40 Tesseract pages, Qwen3 1.7B 22 and 13). Llama 3.2 1B raises CER slightly but significantly on both engines, by 0.039 and 0.033, the dictionary by 0.037 and 0.040, and the four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints by 0.52 to 1.34, on 38 to 40 pages. The instruction-tuned TituLLMs 1B is worse than its base checkpoint, although the two received differently formatted prompts (Section~\ref{sec:correction}). Switching engines changes the size of these effects little: the CER changes of each method differ by at most 0.035 between engines, and the methods keep their order.

Per page, the general-purpose models and the dictionary track the raw OCR closely, so a page's own difficulty sets its score, whereas the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints produce text of similar error whatever the input quality (appendix, Fig.~\ref{fig:corrected}).

The one significant improvement among the 36 tests of the 40-page family is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, whose WER falls from 0.827 to 0.799 ($-0.028$, 95\% CI $[-0.047,-0.013]$, $p=0.002$). Its grapheme-cluster CER also falls, from 0.431 to 0.417 ($p=0.021$ after Holm correction over 18 tests), but its code-point CER does not change significantly ($-0.004$), and on EasyOCR text it improves no metric.

\subsubsection{Robustness of the statistics and the reference}
\label{sec:robustness}
The character-level conclusions do not depend on how the error is measured, how pages are resampled, or how the reference is ordered (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}, appendix). The pooled CER is lower than the mean page CER (0.298 against 0.341 for raw Tesseract output, 0.286 against 0.297 for EasyOCR), because short pages have higher error rates, but it ranks the methods in the same way, and on it no method is below the raw OCR. Grapheme-cluster CER improves only for Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, as reported above. On the bounded cMER (Table~\ref{tab:damage}), 13 of the 18 tests are significant after Holm correction, all increases. Resampling whole books instead of pages gives practically the same intervals, as expected with 37 books for 40 pages.

Against a reference rebuilt in the declared reading order, which changes 17 of the 40 references, the raw OCR scores slightly better (CER 0.335 and 0.294 against 0.341 and 0.297), and the same 12 of the 18 CER comparisons are significant, all increases; Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output stays at $-0.004$ ($p=0.82$). Reference-word recall, which ignores word order and extra text, is 0.431 and 0.484 for the raw OCR. No method raises it significantly: Qwen3 1.7B comes closest ($+0.003$ and $+0.002$), and every other method lowers it significantly, Gemma-2B-it included ($-0.017$ and $-0.019$). The absence of a significant improvement therefore persists under the declared reading order and under order-free word recall, and also when each region is read separately (Section~\ref{sec:region}).

\subsubsection{Region-level OCR}
\label{sec:region}
Reading each annotated region on its own removes out-of-region text and reading-order differences at the source (Section~\ref{sec:further}), and it lowers the input CER. For EasyOCR, region OCR has CER 0.225 against 0.297 for the whole page read in the same run (paired difference $-0.072$, 95\% CI $[-0.120,-0.033]$); for Tesseract, with whole pages and regions read by the same build in the same run, 0.333 against 0.425 ($-0.092$, $[-0.152,-0.029]$). Correction still does not lower CER (Table~\ref{tab:region}). No method lowers CER on either engine. Every method is significantly worse on EasyOCR text, and on Tesseract text all except Qwen3 1.7B ($+0.015$, $p=0.063$) and Qwen3 4B ($+0.009$, $p=0.19$). On whole-page EasyOCR output only Llama 3.2 1B among the general-purpose models was significantly worse; on region text all of them are, adding 0.009 to 0.066 CER. Phi-4-mini's $+0.066$, the widest interval, is driven by three pages whose output reached the token limit: they account for 87\% of its total increase, one of them alone rose by 1.35. Its median page change is $+0.007$, and it is worse on 30 of the 40 pages. A possible reason is that, with fewer errors to repair, their wrong edits weigh more. The dictionary raises CER by 0.036 and 0.044, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints again regenerate the text (CER 0.82 to 1.63). Qwen3 4B lowers WER while raising CER, on EasyOCR text significantly in both directions ($-0.030$ and $+0.014$).

\subsubsection{Synthetic noise}
\label{sec:noise}
If the OCR were simply too noisy to correct, correction should start to help as the noise falls. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:noise}, Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}). On references corrupted to mean CERs of 0.020, 0.048, 0.100, 0.199 and 0.297, each of the four general-purpose models raised the CER at every level, and all 20 increases are significant. Only at the highest level (0.297) did two models lower the word error rate significantly, Llama 3.2 1B by 0.006 and Phi-4-mini by 0.018, while still raising CER. No tested noise level produced a CER reduction: at an input CER of 0.020 the corrected text has CER 0.044 to 0.107, two to five times the input. Gemma-2B-it adds the least, 0.016 to 0.034, and Llama 3.2 1B the most, 0.087 to 0.169 (computed from unrounded values, so differences of the rounded table entries can differ by 0.001). The synthetic noise is not a perfect stand-in for real OCR errors: at the same input CER of about 0.30, the models leave synthetic noise worse than real EasyOCR output (Qwen3 1.7B: 0.362 against 0.315), so the sweep shows the trend, not the exact level a real OCR text would reach.

\subsubsection{Fine-tuning pilot}
\label{sec:finetune}
The pilot saw only about a third of its training pairs once, so we treat it as a diagnostic of an undertrained model; the fully trained correctors of Section~\ref{sec:trained} are the test of task-specific training (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}).
A small model trained for the task behaves much like the larger zero-shot Qwen3 models (Table~\ref{tab:finetune}). The briefly fine-tuned ByT5-small lowers WER significantly on both engines, by 0.024 and 0.037; it fixes 113 and 282 wrong words while breaking 102 and 146 correct ones. CER does not fall. It rises by 0.012 on Tesseract text, significantly, with 25 of the 40 pages worse, and by 0.016 on EasyOCR text, where the bootstrap interval excludes zero but the Holm-corrected test is not significant. On error-free input it does less damage than any other method run on the 40 pages: CER 0.015, against 0.017 for the best dictionary setting and 0.032 for Gemma-2B-it. The checkpoint chosen on the development pages also stayed just above their raw OCR (CER 0.365 against 0.362). This is a preliminary pilot: the model saw about 14{,}300 of its 43{,}312 training pairs, once each, before the time budget ran out. It shows only that this short training produced copy-and-edit behaviour, not how well a fully trained model would correct.

\subsubsection{Methods without a language model}
\label{sec:classical}
Two further methods use no language model (Table~\ref{tab:classical}). A better lexicon and a smaller edit distance reduce the dictionary's damage but do not remove it. The 100{,}000 most frequent words of Bengali Wikipedia miss 37\% of the reference word tokens, against 52\% for the subtitle lexicon. With this lexicon and an edit distance of 1, SymSpell raises CER by 0.007 and 0.008 instead of 0.037 and 0.040, and error-free text reaches CER 0.017 instead of 0.057. Every setting nevertheless remains significantly worse than the raw OCR on both engines: the best one still breaks 211 and 251 correct words and fixes 88 and 201 wrong ones.

We treat both metrics as descriptive because the comparison family was revised after inspecting the first scoring. Like the larger Qwen3 models and the fine-tuned model, fusion fixes more words than it breaks (140 against 71, and 87 against 30), and the margin rule breaks fewer correct words than any language model without gating on the 40 pages. Only the margin rule's change ($-0.002$) reaches $p<0.05$, and only after Holm correction over the two comparisons with EasyOCR ($p=0.045$); over the four comparisons of the first scoring, which also compared fusion with Tesseract output, it does not ($p=0.090$). \subsubsection{Clean-text control}
\label{sec:clean}
Giving the models reference text removes any recognition error they could repair. Nevertheless, every method changes it (Table~\ref{tab:clean}). \subsubsection{Where the character errors go}
\label{sec:editlevel}
Table~\ref{tab:editlevel} splits the change in character errors between the reference words a method fixes, those it breaks, those that stay wrong, and the spaces between words. A fixed word usually saves about one character error: Qwen3 4B and 8B remove 185 to 416 errors by fixing 201 to 340 words, mostly in vowel signs and punctuation. The cost lies elsewhere. The same models edit 394 to 667 words that stay wrong and add 159 to 255 character errors to them, mostly consonants, and the words they break add 89 to 157 more. The fine-tuned pilot shows the same pattern with larger costs. Merged and split words are not the explanation: with all spaces removed before scoring, every CER moves by at most 0.02 and keeps its direction relative to the raw OCR. Engine fusion is the exception: it removes errors from words that stay wrong instead of adding them, which is why it lowers CER slightly.

\subsection{RQ3: Decoding, prompts and safeguards}
\label{sec:rq3}

\subsubsection{Decoding constraints}
\label{sec:decoding}
Table~\ref{tab:decoding} and Fig.~\ref{fig:decoding} compare plain greedy decoding with the repetition penalty alone, the four-gram ban alone and both together (guarded decoding), for the four general-purpose models on the 40 pages, with the OCR text of both engines and with the clean text as input. Every constraint, alone or combined, raised CER in all 33 completed comparisons with plain decoding (Phi-4-mini was not run on EasyOCR text), and all 33 increases are significant after Holm correction. Guarded decoding raised Gemma-2B-it's CER from 0.353 to 0.545 on Tesseract text, Qwen3 1.7B's from 0.337 to 0.925 and Phi-4-mini's from 0.354 to 1.740, so the three models that leave the OCR almost unchanged under plain decoding become clearly harmful. The clean-text control shows the same effect without any recognition error to repair: guarded decoding raised the CER of error-free input from 0.032 to 0.360 for Gemma-2B-it, from 0.035 to 0.940 for Qwen3 1.7B and from 0.060 to 1.406 for Phi-4-mini. The constrained outputs really are worse; what would mislead is to attribute that loss to the checkpoints' ability to correct, since the same checkpoints barely change their input under plain decoding. This comparison alone does not show why the constraints do this: besides acting on the prompt, a four-gram ban can block repetitions that the reference text itself contains, and a penalty can suppress valid repeated tokens. We therefore reran the four models on Tesseract and clean text with each constraint restricted to the generated tokens (Table~\ref{tab:decodinggen}). Restricting them removes much of the damage: the generated-only constraints are significantly less harmful than the whole-sequence ones in 21 of 24 settings, and with both constraints between 20\% and 91\% of the extra error disappears; Phi-4-mini's CER on Tesseract text falls from 1.740 to 0.688. Acting on the prompt is therefore a large part of the problem, but not all of it: the generated-only constraints are still significantly worse than plain decoding in 23 of 24 settings. The four-gram ban on the output alone adds 0.017 to 0.099 CER on Tesseract text, whereas the penalty on the output alone still adds 0.275 to 0.404 for Qwen3 1.7B, Phi-4-mini and Llama 3.2 1B (Gemma-2B-it: $+0.002$), so it also suppresses repetitions that the Bengali text genuinely contains. For copy-heavy correction, plain decoding is the safe choice.

Which constraint does the damage depends on the model. For Gemma-2B-it almost all of it comes from the $n$-gram ban ($+0.176$ on Tesseract text, against $+0.004$ for the penalty). For Phi-4-mini the penalty alone is catastrophic ($+1.396$, with 28 of 40 pages reaching the token limit) and the $n$-gram ban moderate ($+0.155$). For Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B each constraint alone raises CER by 0.35 to 0.49 on OCR text and by 0.59 to 0.69 on clean text. An evaluation that uses either constraint would thus misjudge a copying model, and testing only one of them would not show the size of the problem for every model. An earlier guarded-versus-plain comparison on the 15-page CPU sample (table note) also included two Bengali-pretrained checkpoints: guarded decoding raised the CER of the decoder-only TituLLMs 1B by more than 1, but not that of BanglaT5, an encoder-decoder model whose decoder does not see the input tokens as its own history.

\subsubsection{Confidence gating}
\label{sec:gating}
Confidence gating (Table~\ref{tab:gated}) works at the word level instead of the page level. On EasyOCR output, where confidence is per box and therefore coarse, no gated method improves on the raw OCR. The thresholds were fixed in advance, not chosen on these pages, but the development pages have no confidence scores from this OCR run, so the gain could not be validated on held-out pages; we report it as exploratory. Lower thresholds, which allow fewer changes, give the lowest error throughout.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The practical question is whether the corrected text is better than the raw OCR it would replace. The answer depends on the metric: some models repair whole words while adding character errors elsewhere.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\textbf{RQ1.} Character- and word-level outcomes differ. On the held-out pages, the confirmatory test, no zero-shot method lowered per-block CER and 17 of the 18 CER tests showed a significant increase; Gemma-2B-it and Qwen3 1.7B lowered WER on Tesseract text (Table~\ref{tab:testzeroshot}). The 40-page evaluation points the same way. There, no ungated model significantly reduced mean code-point CER, bounded cMER or pooled CER on either engine (Tables~\ref{tab:matrix}, \ref{tab:robustness} and~\ref{tab:scale}), nor on region-level OCR (Table~\ref{tab:region}); the 10-page intervals of Qwen3 32B include zero (Table~\ref{tab:large}). On the grapheme-cluster CER, Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract text is the one significant exception (Section~\ref{sec:main}). The word error rate behaves differently: Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract text, Qwen3 4B and 8B and the fine-tuned ByT5-small on both engines, and Llama 3.2 3B on EasyOCR text lower it significantly, as do Qwen3 4B on EasyOCR region text and two models at the highest synthetic noise level. The only significant CER reduction is exploratory and small, about 1\% of the raw CER: Qwen3 1.7B with confidence gating at one of three thresholds (Table~\ref{tab:gated}). Engine fusion gains about as much, but we report this gain as descriptive, not significant (Table~\ref{tab:classical}). The engines and the two OCR settings support the same broad character-level conclusion; the region extension remains exploratory because crop settings used evaluation pages.

\textbf{RQ2.} Repairs often came at the cost of text that was already correct. Every method changed error-free text, on the held-out pages (Tables~\ref{tab:testzeroshot} and~\ref{tab:testtrained}) as on the 40 pages (Tables~\ref{tab:clean}, \ref{tab:scale} and~\ref{tab:finetune}); on the 40 pages the change ranged from CER 0.015 for the fine-tuned model to more than 0.8 for the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, which regenerate the text and lose most correct words. The general-purpose core models keep most of their input, and the attribution maps on sampled chunks are consistent with copying (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}, appendix); they repair few words, and only Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract text repairs more than it breaks. The larger Qwen3 models and the fine-tuned model repair more words than they break, but their other edits add at least as many character errors as the repairs remove (Table~\ref{tab:editlevel}).

\textbf{RQ3.} Repetition constraints that include the prompt changed the outcome most: alone or together they made every general-purpose model significantly worse, on OCR text and on error-free text (Table~\ref{tab:decoding}). Restricted to generated tokens they did much less damage, but still significantly more than plain decoding in all but one setting (Table~\ref{tab:decodinggen}). Float32 precision and a completion prompt did not bring a Bengali-pretrained checkpoint near the raw OCR (Table~\ref{tab:checks}). No tested prompt variant, chunking scheme or page-level safeguard produced a statistically significant CER reduction relative to raw OCR (Tables~\ref{tab:variants} and~\ref{tab:safeguards}), although some lowered mean CER slightly (for example 0.335 against 0.341 for Qwen3 1.7B with the preserve-spelling instruction on Tesseract text). Confidence gating and engine fusion reduced the damage, with the small gains noted under RQ1. % GATING-PENDING: add the held-out gating result here

\textbf{RQ4.} No. Trained for the task on aligned OCR pairs and synthetic errors and chosen on validation pages, ByT5-small and BanglaByT5 raised CER on Tesseract text significantly and did not lower it on EasyOCR text (Table~\ref{tab:testtrained}). Like the larger zero-shot models, they repaired whole words, and lowered EasyOCR WER significantly, but did not turn those repairs into a character-level gain. Descriptively, their CER was higher than that of the copying zero-shot models (we did not test this comparison), and they changed more error-free text than the best of those. A fallback to the raw OCR on suspect blocks removed their damage without adding a gain. Unlike the unadapted Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, the trained models edit the text instead of regenerating it: on error-free input their CER was 0.113 and 0.085, against 0.96 or more for those checkpoints. Their edits still do not make the text better than the raw OCR.

\subsection{Word-level gains without character-level gains}
WER records whether a word is exactly right; it does not distinguish a word with one wrong sign from a word with several wrong characters. CER counts those differences, as well as insertions and deletions. In our edit-level analysis (Section~\ref{sec:editlevel}), repaired words were mostly one sign away from correct. The larger Qwen3 models and the fine-tuned model fixed more words than they broke, but they also changed consonants in words that remained wrong. Those changes, together with damage to correct words, offset the character errors removed by the repairs. Whole-word search might benefit from some of these repairs, but faithful transcription requires preservation at the character level too. We did not evaluate search or other downstream uses.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Failures of zero-shot correction have also been reported for Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, historical transcripts in several languages~\cite{boros2024postcorrection} and Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Our clean-text control measures the over-correction reported in HIPE-OCRepair~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and for GPT models on historical English~\cite{zhang2024prosody}. Positive results have involved task-specific training~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}, a frontier model~\cite{viana2026reframing}, or nearly clean English~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Our whole-page input CER of 0.30 to 0.34 is higher than the maximum in those studies, 0.254. At that level, GPT-4o-mini lowered CER slightly to 0.250, while a 70-billion-parameter model raised it~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Input quality alone does not account for our results, however. The 70-billion-parameter model helped at CER 0.07~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, whereas our small models raised CER even on synthetic input at 0.02 and on region-level OCR at 0.23 and 0.33. Our decoding results also show why a negative evaluation needs a plain-decoding comparison: the constraints themselves can make a copying model look much worse.

\subsection{Possible explanations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Input noise.} The models failed on more than the noisiest pages. Every method damaged error-free input, every general-purpose model raised CER at all five synthetic noise levels down to 0.020 (Section~\ref{sec:noise}), and correction was more consistently harmful on region-level OCR than on whole-page OCR despite its lower input CER (Section~\ref{sec:region}). This leaves room for effects of historical vocabulary and the structure or combination of errors. Our synthetic model only approximates real OCR errors, which the correctors handle somewhat better at the same CER.
\item \emph{Model size.} The Qwen3 series repairs more words as size increases from 0.6B to 8B: fixed words come to outnumber broken words, and WER falls. CER does not fall. The 32B model also changes clean text more than the smaller Qwen3 models (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Scaling up to 32 billion parameters in 4-bit therefore did not give a zero-shot CER improvement in these experiments.
\item \emph{Task-specific training.} Lack of training does not explain the result on its own. Two byte-level models trained for the task on aligned OCR pairs and synthetic errors, with checkpoints chosen on validation pages, did not lower CER on the held-out pages, and on Tesseract text raised it significantly (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}). Their validation CER stopped improving before one pass over the training data while training loss still fell, which suggests that the training pairs, about 1{,}200 real pieces from 62 pages and synthetic noise, do not teach what the test pages need. Larger sets of real OCR pairs might, but none exists for historical Bengali print.
\item \emph{Tokenisation.} English-centric tokenizers use several times as many tokens for Bengali as for English~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer,ahia2023cost}. Our measured range is 0.29 to 1.22 tokens per character (Table~\ref{tab:cost}), but token fertility does not explain the ordering of results. Llama 3.2 1B and TituLLMs 1B share a tokenizer and the highest fertility; the former stays near the baseline while the latter triples its error rate. This comparison also changes instruction tuning and pretraining, so it shows that fertility alone is insufficient, not which training choice caused the difference.
\item \emph{Historical spelling.} A model or lexicon built for modern Bengali may mistake older spellings for errors. Our subtitle dictionary replaces about 31 correct words per page of clean text, and half of the reference words are missing from it. A Wikipedia lexicon, which misses about a third of them, together with a smaller edit distance, cuts the dictionary's damage by four-fifths but does not remove it (Section~\ref{sec:classical}). Asking models to preserve spelling slightly reduces damage in seven of the eight general-purpose model-and-engine pairs. Mixing old and modern literary registers also caused difficulty for GPT-4o in grammar correction~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. Historical spelling is therefore part of the explanation for the dictionary; for the language models we have not tested its effect directly. Absence from a modern lexicon alone would not identify an archaic spelling: names, rare words and tokenisation also cause out-of-vocabulary forms.
\item \emph{Prompt format and instruction-following.} The instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints often describe the input or return nothing rather than correct it. The prompt format explains only part of this: a completion prompt lowered the base checkpoint's CER by about 0.2 but left it far above the raw OCR, and normalising BanglaT5's input changed nothing (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). Instruction tuning that did not cover copy-and-edit tasks is a plausible cause; the completion-prompt checks on the instruction-tuned checkpoints also left both far worse than the raw OCR (Section~\ref{sec:precision}).
\end{itemize}
These observations concern the tested checkpoints and configurations. Other Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, more training data or real OCR pairs could give different results.

\subsection{Practical implications}
For an institution processing similar historical Bengali books, the first step is to retain the raw OCR alongside any corrected version and check correction against reference transcriptions. Skipping correction saves two to fourteen minutes of CPU time per page in our 15-page timing sample (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). If correction is attempted, word confidence can limit damage: gating reduced the harm of every method that had increased error, and fusion broke the fewest correct words without a language model or GPU. Their gains were only about 1\% of raw CER on the 40 pages. % GATING-PENDING: say whether the gating gain held on the held-out pages
Training a small corrector on the available data did not help either. Larger Qwen3 models and the word-level gains of the trained correctors may be worth testing where whole-word accuracy matters, but their WER gains do not establish better search performance; we did not measure that. For the tested copy-heavy correction settings, plain decoding is preferable to repetition constraints, including those restricted to generated tokens.
\subsection{Threats to validity and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sample size and statistics.} With 41 held-out pages from 36 book groups, and 40 pages from 37 books before them, the intervals are moderately wide. Only the 36 CER and WER tests of the held-out zero-shot evaluation are confirmatory, with the 8 tests of the trained correctors fixed in advance beside them; every other test is exploratory, Holm correction holds only within each family, and the 10-page comparison of the 32-billion-parameter model provides descriptive estimates with wide intervals and limited power; non-significance does not establish the absence of a useful effect.
\item \emph{Reference coverage and order.} The engines read the whole page, while the ground truth covers the annotated regions in file order. A reference in the declared reading order, an order-free word recall and region-level OCR gave the same conclusions, but the region-level Tesseract text comes from a later build whose output differs from the main run, so it is compared only with whole-page text of that build and never mixed with the main run's text.
\item \emph{Reuse of data.} The few-shot examples, the noise model and the fine-tuning checkpoint come from the 10 development pages, which were also used for debugging. The extensions were added after the 40-page evaluation and reuse its pages; the region crop settings were compared on 8 of them, and the fusion test family was fixed after a first scoring. The guarded decoding setting was chosen after seeing development output. For these reasons the 40-page results are exploratory, and the held-out test, scored once with settings fixed in advance, carries the confirmatory claims. The held-out test does not cover everything: the larger models (Qwen3 4B, 8B and 32B), the prompt variants, the region and noise extensions and engine fusion were run on the 40 pages only, so the word-level gains of the larger Qwen3 models rest on those reused pages. The trained correctors' training pages include 33 of the 40 evaluation pages, which is harmless for the held-out test but means they cannot be scored on those 40 pages. The guard of the trained correctors was written after reading validation outputs and is reported as secondary.
\item \emph{Model and prompt fit.} The results describe the tested checkpoint and prompt combinations, not Bengali pretraining in general. BanglaT5 is an unfine-tuned span denoiser and TituLLMs 1B a base causal model; their objectives differ from those of the instruction-tuned checkpoints. Comparisons between these groups do not isolate the effect of Bengali pretraining: the instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints received their shipped template and, on Tesseract text only, a completion prompt; no output filter removed preambles (which penalises the TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints most; the copying models add almost none), and Phi-4-mini was rerun in float32 on Tesseract text only.
\item \emph{Environment.} PyTorch and CUDA versions of the main runs and the Leptonica version of the OCR were not recorded, and the OCR could not be regenerated a week later; the released outputs, not a rerun, are the reference for every reported score. Rescoring them is pinned and reproducible; regenerating them is not guaranteed.
\item \emph{Metric scope.} We measured string agreement with the reference, not search, information extraction or a human corrector's time. WER and CER can disagree, as they do for the larger models.
\item \emph{External validity.} The pages are early printed books from one library's collections, chosen by whether a transcription exists; the held-out pages come from other books but from the same British Library holdings and period, so the test is book-disjoint, not collection-disjoint. The 40 pages were labelled by page type by one annotator, so the page-type figures are descriptive. The results may not transfer to modern print or other collections. They also cover only the tested sizes: up to 8 billion parameters on 40 pages and one 32-billion-parameter model on 10, so they say nothing about frontier-scale models.
\end{itemize}
Held-out pages are still needed to check whether the small fusion gain persists. % GATING-PENDING: gating sentence
The trained correctors were each trained once, with one seed; more seeds, more real OCR pairs and richer corruption models would show whether training can help where these runs did not. Since WER and CER disagree, future evaluations should measure what correction changes for search, extraction or a human corrector's workload. Other collections, larger models and multimodal transcription would test how far these findings extend. Bengali byte-level and Indic sequence-to-sequence backbones are relevant alternatives; confusion-weighted lexical correction would test a different classical approach from uniform edit-distance lookup.

\section{Conclusion}

On 41 held-out pages of historical Bengali print, from books not used before and with reading order reviewed by hand, no zero-shot method lowered per-block CER for either OCR engine, and most raised it significantly. Two correctors trained for the task and chosen on separate validation pages did not lower it either. The 40-page evaluation gave the same answer, and there the tested models also failed to lower CER on region-level OCR and on synthetic noise down to an input CER of 0.02. There were word-level gains: on the held-out pages Gemma-2B-it and Qwen3 1.7B lowered WER on Tesseract text and both trained correctors on EasyOCR text, and on the 40 pages Qwen3 4B and 8B repaired more words than they broke. Edits elsewhere offset those repairs at the character level. Every method changed error-free input, and repetition constraints substantially worsened models that otherwise mostly copied the source; excluding prompt tokens reduced that harm without removing it. % GATING-PENDING: gating and fusion sentence
These results support retaining raw OCR and testing any replacement against reference transcriptions on comparable material. They leave open what larger sets of real OCR pairs, other Bengali collections and downstream evaluation might show.

\subsection{Precision and prompt format}
\label{sec:precision}
Two choices in the main runs could have made the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints look worse than they are: the numerical precision of the two largest models, and the prompt format (Table~\ref{tab:checks}).

\textbf{Precision.} TituLLMs 3B Instruct, run in float16, returned an empty page in 43 of its 120 outputs, each within 0.2 to 0.5~s, so it ended generation at or near its first token. Float16 overflow could cause this, so we reran it in float32 and recorded its first-token logits in both precisions. The first token was the same on all 208 chunks, and the end-of-sequence token on the same 87; no value was infinite or NaN, and the largest activation, about 1{,}000, was far below the float16 limit of 65{,}504. The float32 run returned the same empty pages and changed mean CER by at most 0.002 on OCR text and by $-0.040$ on the clean text, not significantly (95\% CI $[-0.132,+0.032]$), and Phi-4-mini in float32 gave the same CER on Tesseract text (0.354). The empty pages are the model's own behaviour, not a precision artefact.

\textbf{Prompt format.} The base TituLLMs 1B checkpoint received our instruction as plain text with no cue for where its answer should begin. With a completion-style prompt that ends with such a cue, its CER fell from 1.084 to 0.877 on Tesseract text, from 1.033 to 0.861 on EasyOCR text and from 0.988 to 0.605 on the clean text (Wilcoxon signed-rank, unadjusted $p<0.001$ in each case; exploratory), but it remains far worse than the raw OCR ($+0.54$ and $+0.56$ CER on OCR text). Passing BanglaT5's input through the normaliser its model card asks for changed its CER by at most 0.002. On Tesseract text, the completion prompt also lowers TituLLMs 1B Instruct CER from 1.642 to 1.336 ($p=0.014$, Holm over the two Instruct checks), whereas 3B Instruct changes from 0.958 to 0.960 without a significant difference. These later checks are separate from the base-model and normalisation rows in Table~\ref{tab:checks}; both Instruct outputs remain far worse than raw OCR. The prompt format thus matters, but these checks do not change the conclusion: every rerun of a Bengali-pretrained checkpoint stays significantly worse than the raw OCR.

\subsection{Edit profiles by model}
\label{sec:profiles}

\emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini leave substitutions, deletions and insertions close to the raw OCR's counts (on Tesseract text, Gemma-2B-it: 93, 76 and 64 against 92, 60 and 69). Llama 3.2 1B mainly deletes: deletions rise from 60 to 110 per page on Tesseract text and from 58 to 93 on EasyOCR text.

\emph{Dictionary baseline.} SymSpell adds substitutions (92 to 111 and 84 to 108). In the first example it replaces a historical form with a modern one, and pages with more words missing from its lexicon lose more correct words (see \emph{Lexicon coverage}); we did not check how many of its substitutions replace valid historical spellings rather than non-words.

\emph{BanglaT5.} Its output is on average 0.45 times the reference length, deletions rise to 503 and 434 per page, and 28\% of its non-space characters are punctuation, ASCII digits or other symbols. Like TituLLMs 3B Instruct, it rarely reaches the generation limit (at most 25\% of pages), so its errors do not come from truncation.

\emph{TituLLMs.} The three checkpoints fail in different ways. The base 1B model substitutes and inserts, reached the generation limit on every page, and repeats a segment of at least 20 characters three or more times in 51 of its 80 outputs. The 1B Instruct checkpoint writes 2.09 times the reference length, a third of it in runs of at least six characters absent from the input. The 3B Instruct checkpoint returned an empty page in 27 of its 80 outputs, and in 37 described the text (for example, that it appears to be a copy of a historical document) instead of correcting it.

\emph{Character classes.} The raw OCR is least accurate on Bengali digits (65\% and 53\% of reference digits wrong), the danda (59\% for Tesseract; EasyOCR never outputs one) and punctuation, and most accurate on consonants (19\% and 14\%). The general-purpose models raise the error rate of every class slightly. The largest change, Gemma-2B-it on digits (65\% to 75\% and 53\% to 73\%), is mostly a script conversion: it rewrites Bengali digits as ASCII digits (88 to 336 ASCII digits on Tesseract text), but mapping ASCII to Bengali digits before scoring changes its CER by only 0.002 and 0.006. Removing zero-width joiners and non-joiners changes any CER by at most 0.003, and normalising khanda-ta changes nothing.

\emph{Lexicon coverage.} 52\% of the Bengali word tokens in the references are missing from the dictionary's subtitle lexicon (37\% from a Wikipedia lexicon, Section~\ref{sec:classical}). On pages with more such words, SymSpell breaks a larger share of the words the OCR had right (Spearman $\rho=0.38$ on both engines, $p<0.02$).

\emph{Page types.} Lists (contents, indexes, tables) are the hardest pages for both engines (raw CER 0.576 and 0.511) and prose the easiest (0.199 and 0.184; Table~\ref{tab:pagetypes}). No method improves any page type consistently; with 8 to 13 pages per type, these figures are descriptive.

\subsection{Gradient-based attribution of generated tokens}
\label{sec:attribution}
The output analyses above show copying and regeneration directly. Token-level Grad-CAM maps (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}) add supplementary evidence about which input positions each generated token is sensitive to; they are not attention weights and do not establish a causal explanation. They cover one chunk from each of 10 pages, up to 24 generated tokens per chunk (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}, Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}). The general-purpose models copy 90\% to 99\% of their output tokens from the input text, and for 58\% to 81\% of the copied tokens the element-wise map at mid depth peaks on the source token itself, against a chance rate of 1\% to 2\%. Their maps put about half of their mass on the input text and most of the rest on their own earlier output. In Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution} this shows as a diagonal: the map of each token Gemma-2B-it copies peaks at or beside its source. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints copy only 3\% to 25\% of their output tokens, and their maps are spread over the prompt. TituLLMs 1B Instruct puts more of its map on its own earlier output (0.55 and 0.53) than on the OCR text (0.28 and 0.31), which fits its long, repetitive and invented output described above. The few tokens the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints do copy often peak on their source too (20\% to 68\% at mid depth), so the difference lies mainly in how much they copy, not in how they copy. The maps look much the same with Tesseract text and with clean text as input.

\centering

\caption{Element-wise Grad-CAM maps at mid depth for one chunk of Tesseract text: each row is a generated token, each column an OCR token of the prompt, darker means a larger share of the map (each row scaled to its own maximum). Literal Grad-CAM does not localise: its map peaks on the copied source token for only 0\% to 8\% of copied tokens, close to chance. Averaging the gradient over all positions, as Grad-CAM does for the channels of a CNN, keeps one weight per hidden dimension and discards which position the score depends on; the same weights then highlight every token whose hidden state resembles the source. The element-wise variant keeps the gradient at each position and so avoids this failure.

\subsection{Page-level safeguards}
\label{sec:safeguards}
The length rule (Table~\ref{tab:safeguards}) replaces outputs only for three Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, on 5 to 23 of the 40 pages. This lowers their CER, for example from 0.958 to 0.660 for TituLLMs 3B Instruct on Tesseract output, but each remains significantly worse than the raw OCR (there $+0.319$, 95\% CI $[+0.193,+0.460]$, $p=0.001$ after Holm correction over 18 tests); the other models never trigger the rule. The edit-distance cap brings no method significantly below the raw OCR at any $\tau$ (lowest: Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, 0.337 against 0.341, not significant); seven capped results are significantly worse, and for the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints the cap keeps the raw OCR on all but at most two pages.


\subsection{CPU and GPU runs}
\label{sec:cpugpu}
On the five pages whose EasyOCR text was byte-identical between the CPU and the GPU runs, Gemma-2B-it produced the same output on all five, and the outputs of Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B differed from their CPU counterparts by a CER of at most 0.004. The outputs of TituLLMs 1B and BanglaT5 differed more (CER 0.20 and 0.44 between runs), but their scores agreed (0.945 against 0.954 and 0.806 against 0.813). The check cannot isolate device effects from precision effects: hardware and precision change together (bfloat16 on the CPU, float32 on the GPU), only five EasyOCR pages qualify, no Tesseract page does because its text changed between the two OCR runs, and the two float16 models were never run on the CPU.
