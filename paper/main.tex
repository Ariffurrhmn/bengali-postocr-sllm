\begin{abstract}
Historical Bengali print remains difficult for optical character recognition (OCR), and small language models are a possible low-cost way to correct its output without retraining. We used eight open models of 0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters and a dictionary spell-checker, zero-shot, to correct Tesseract and EasyOCR output on 40 pages printed between 1713 and 1914. No method significantly reduced the character error rate (CER) of either engine. The general-purpose models mostly copied their input. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints we tested, prompted through their chat templates, regenerated or dropped the text and raised CER above 0.8. Given the error-free reference as input, every method still changed it. A common decoding default, a repetition penalty with an n-gram ban, also acts on the prompt; in an ablation it made copying models appear harmful, raising CER by up to 1.6. Confidence gating, which keeps only edits to words the OCR engine was unsure of, removed most of the damage, but its one significant gain, 0.004 CER (about 1\%) for Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, is negligible and not robust. Fine-tuned and larger models were not tested.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Bengali OCR, post-OCR correction, small language models, zero-shot prompting, historical documents, low-resource NLP, negative results

\section{Introduction}

Bengali has about 278 million speakers and ranks seventh by total speakers~\cite{eberhard2025ethnologue}. Turning its printed records into searchable text remains difficult. The bbOCR evaluation reports an average CER of 0.78 for Tesseract across Bengali document types, compared with 0.59 for the purpose-built bbOCR system~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}. Another study reports Tesseract character accuracy ranging from 37 to 84 percent across document types~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Small differences in strokes distinguish many Bengali characters. Conjunct glyphs combine consonants, and vowel signs can sit before, after, above or below the consonant they modify~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Worn type, uneven inking and ornate display lettering add to the difficulty of historical books; training corpora for recognition are also small~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}.

Post-correction works on the recognised text, so it can be used without modifying or retraining the OCR engine. A text-only correction step could receive output from any engine. Small models are of interest because their lower hardware requirements may suit archives with limited computing resources~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}, including national archives, government offices and non-governmental organisations digitising Bengali documents.

Whether language-model correction helps depends on the setting. Task-specific fine-tuning has improved OCR text~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,beshirov2025bulgarian}, as have large models given nearly clean English input~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Zero-shot use has produced mixed results: open models helped English but not Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, and only one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models improved an English newspaper corpus~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. Failures have been reported with noisier input and less-resourced languages. Even a 70-billion-parameter model raised error rates once input CER reached about 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; zero-shot models also increased the word error rate of Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Bengali OCR correction has used lexicons, morphology and edit distance~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}. A commercial language model has also cleaned OCR text from modern textbooks during benchmark construction~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. We found no controlled study of zero-shot open models below four billion parameters on historical Bengali print that both compares one correction step across engines and checks whether generation settings account for the reported failure. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can small open language models (0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters), prompted zero-shot, reduce the character and word error rates of OCR output for historical Bengali print?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Is the effect of correction consistent across OCR engines when the correction step is not adapted to either engine?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} What does correction do to the text: does it repair recognition errors, or does it change text that was already correct?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Do the decoding setting, the prompt, the chunking or a safeguard change the outcome?
\end{itemize}

As prompted, the models did not significantly reduce CER. Their outputs ranged from largely unchanged OCR to regenerated text, and all methods altered some correct text. We report the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A decoding pitfall for evaluations of correction: a repetition penalty and an n-gram ban, common defaults against repetition loops, also act on the prompt in a decoder-only model, and they make a model that mostly copies its input appear to damage it.
\item A controlled evaluation of zero-shot post-correction on historical Bengali print: eight open models below four billion parameters, two OCR engines and a dictionary baseline on 40 pages whose split was frozen before any model was run, with paired bootstrap intervals, Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests and equivalence tests.
\item An analysis of what correction changes and whether it can be contained: a clean-text control, correct words broken and wrong words fixed, copying versus regeneration, prompt and chunking variants, page-level safeguards, and edits restricted to words the OCR engine was unsure of.
\item Public code, page lists and prompts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

The relevant work covers recognition, correction of its output, and Bengali models and tokenisation. We also consider grammar and spelling correction in Bengali and other Indic languages.

\subsection{Bengali OCR systems}
Bengali OCR research has mostly addressed recognition itself. bbOCR combines detection, a Bengali recogniser and layout reconstruction to make whole documents searchable; its evaluation compares it only with Tesseract, reporting average text-level CER of 0.59 and 0.78, respectively~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}. Document type matters: Rabby et al.\ train separate word-segmentation models for computer-composed, letterpress, typewritten and handwritten material, obtaining character accuracy of 83 to 90 percent compared with Tesseract's 37 to 84 percent~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Other work operates at different scales. Safir et al.\ recognise single handwritten words without surrounding context, with CER 0.091 and WER 0.273~\cite{safir2021endtoend}. Chaudhury et al.\ address degraded printed Bangla by separating text from background with a generative adversarial network, then using an encoder-decoder recogniser designed to reduce word error rate~\cite{chaudhury2022deep}. These approaches improve recognition but leave errors on difficult or degraded pages. None supplies a separate correction step for those errors.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction before language models}
Nguyen et al.'s survey describes post-OCR methods developed before large language models and notes the poorer recognition quality of historical material~\cite{nguyen2021survey}. Shared benchmarks came from the ICDAR competitions: about 12 million characters of English and French monographs and periodicals spanning four centuries in 2017~\cite{chiron2017icdar}, then about 22 million characters in ten European languages in 2019, where the best system improved the text by 44\%~\cite{rigaud2019icdar}. Copying is useful in low-resource correction. A model that can copy characters from first-pass OCR reduced error rates by an average of 34\% across three endangered languages~\cite{rijhwani2020ocr}. Bengali approaches have relied on lexical resources. Pal et al.\ parse words into roots and suffixes for lexicon lookup~\cite{pal2000ocr}; Ahmed et al.\ use edit distance and character n-grams with about 541{,}000 dictionary words, lowering error rates by about 3\% on 50 documents~\cite{ahmed2016improving}. Such methods require aligned OCR/reference pairs or language-specific lexicons. We know of no comparable resource for historical Bengali print, hence our interest in models that require no task-specific training.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction with language models}
Fine-tuning and zero-shot prompting have produced different outcomes. On short segments of 19th-century English newspapers, a fine-tuned Llama 2 reduced CER by 54.5 percent, compared with 23.3 percent for fine-tuned BART~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}. Without task-specific tuning, success varies by language and input quality. In Kanerva et al.'s English and Finnish evaluation, most open-weight models of 8 to 70 billion parameters improved English, but every one made Finnish worse; only GPT-4o, evaluated alongside them, improved Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. Koynov and Doan show how this can change with noise: zero-shot GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 70B lowered newspaper CER by 48 to 58 percent when input CER was 0.07, but raised it by about 19 percent at 0.15. Llama also raised German CER by 41 percent at an input CER of 0.25~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. On an English newspaper corpus with input CER 0.084, only one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models helped; the others increased CER, one five-fold~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. A study of instruction-tuned German models likewise found no reduction in average periodical CER, although the best model reduced WER by 22 percent. Its re-analysis of an earlier study found the greatest degradation on inputs longer than about 400 characters~\cite{danilova2025german}.

The 2026 HIPE-OCRepair task compared zero-shot, fine-tuned and continued-pretraining systems of 8 to 24 billion parameters on English, French and German. The best combined continued pre-training with fine-tuning and outperformed the zero-shot systems; over-correction of accurate text was a recurring failure~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Other successful results use different conditions. A frontier model reduced CER on Portuguese survey pages from 11.7 to 4.7 percent~\cite{viana2026reframing}, while a supervised two-stage system improved historical Bulgarian but was specific to its language and engine~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}. Direct multimodal transcription of 18th-century Russian sometimes beat conventional OCR, yet also introduced archaic characters from the wrong period~\cite{levchenko2025historical}. Modern-language spelling correction can itself be unsuitable for historical text: Do et al.\ attribute part of its poor performance to the mismatch between modern and historical corpora and instead anchor a language model to reference text~\cite{do2025reference}. In the studies reviewed here, zero-shot correction helped mainly with large or frontier models and input CER below about 0.15. Damage to already accurate text remains a common problem.

\subsection{Bengali language models and tokenisation}
Bengali models differ in whether they can generate text at all. BanglaBERT is an encoder for understanding tasks and cannot rewrite OCR output~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}; BanglaT5 is a sequence-to-sequence generation model~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5}. The TituLLMs family uses Bengali-pretrained Llama 3.2 models at 1B and 3B, trained on about 37 billion tokens with an extended tokenizer~\cite{nahin2025titullms}. BanglaLlama continues pretraining other Llama models. Its authors report that Bengali and Hindi together account for less than 0.21 percent of Llama 2's pretraining tokens, compared with 89.7 percent for English~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Limited exposure is only part of the concern. Mahfuz et al.\ find that inefficient Bengali tokenisation increases computation and reduces generation quality when assessing the need for dedicated Bengali models~\cite{mahfuz2025toolate}. Across languages, Petrov et al.\ show that English-centric tokenizers require several times more tokens for equivalent content; Bengali needs roughly five to ten times the English count, compared with about 1.4 to 1.6 times for multilingual XLM-R and M2M100 tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer}. Ahia et al.\ also associate over-segmentation with higher cost and poorer model performance~\cite{ahia2023cost}. General multilingual models therefore face both limited Bengali exposure and inefficient tokenisation. To our knowledge, Bengali-pretrained models have not been evaluated on OCR correction.

\subsection{Correction of Bengali and other Indic text}
The closest Bengali language-model correction study concerns grammar rather than OCR. Instruction-tuning improved its scores by 3 to 7 points over zero-shot use, though humans still did better. Mixing the older literary register with the modern register was among GPT-4o's hardest error categories~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. In Hindi speech-recognition correction, fine-tuned models of 0.3 to 0.58 billion parameters outperformed much larger models. Zero-shot GPT-4o-mini increased WER from 28.6 to 31.8 and from 18.0 to 25.1 percent; the authors attribute this to over-correction that grows with model size~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Supervised Bengali spelling systems include BSpell, which combines BERT and a convolutional sub-network and outperforms earlier checkers on two Bengali datasets and one Hindi dataset~\cite{rahman2023bspell}, and a transformer detector-purificator-corrector for Bengali and other resource-scarce Indic languages~\cite{bijoy2025transformer}. BnMMLU provides an example of language-model use after Bengali OCR: GPT-3.5 copy-edited EasyOCR output from modern textbooks and exam guides during dataset preparation. The authors report fewer formatting and spelling errors and manually check about 10\% of the items, but give no reference-based error rate for that step~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. These studies cover modern grammar and spelling, or unmeasured cleaning of modern print. We found no evaluation of language-model OCR correction on historical Bengali print.

\subsection{Research gap}
Table~\ref{tab:literature} compares the language-model studies. Existing Bengali OCR correction uses lexicons, morphology and edit distance~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}, or a commercial model for cleaning without reference-based evaluation~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. None of the reviewed work measures zero-shot language-model correction of historical Bengali print. The reviewed post-OCR studies also do not evaluate open models below seven billion parameters, input CER above 0.25, or one correction step across OCR engines. Our evaluation addresses these gaps in language, scale and engine coverage.

\section{Methodology}

We score raw and corrected OCR against the same ground truth. The protocol below specifies the corpus, recognition and correction steps, model selection, classical baseline and evaluation.

\subsection{Overview}
Each page passes through two OCR engines, then through a correction model in chunks (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). We score both versions of the text against its manual transcription. Keeping recognition and correction separate lets the same correction step accept output from any engine. All models are used as released, without training or fine-tuning.

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the ground-truth transcriptions released with the ICDAR 2019 Competition on Recognition of Early Indian Printed Documents (REID2019)~\cite{clausner2019reid}, produced by the British Library's Two Centuries of Indian Print project with the PRImA Research Lab and Jadavpur University. We use version~2 of the British Library's release, whose XML coordinates were improved~\cite{britishlibrary2019bengaligt}. The source volumes are Bengali books printed between 1713 and 1914. The digitised images are out of copyright and the transcriptions are released under the Public Domain Mark.

The release contains 81 page images with PAGE-XML annotations. Twenty-nine of them (36\%) have text regions marked but no transcribed text, so no error rate can be computed, and they are excluded; 52 pages remain. We drop the two pages with the thinnest transcriptions, measured by the share of text regions that actually contain text, which leaves a pool of 50 pages. The pool is split into 10 development pages and 40 evaluation pages, and the split was frozen in saved page-identifier lists and committed to version control before any correction model was run. The development pages were used only to debug the pipeline and as the source of the worked examples in one prompt variant; no development page contributes to any reported score. All 40 evaluation pages are scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). They come from 37 books, with at most two pages from any one book (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The release gives no publication date per book. Each page was labelled by its main content, from its transcription, by a single annotator without a second check: 10 title pages, often in ornate display type, 8 verse pages (including verse followed by a commentary), 13 prose pages, and 9 lists (tables of contents, indexes, tables and dictionary entries); the labels are released with the code. A random sample of 15 of the evaluation pages (seed 403) was also processed on a CPU; it provides the decoding ablation and the CPU timings.

\subsection{Baseline OCR}
\label{sec:ocr}
Two open-source engines produce the noisy text. Tesseract~5.3.4~\cite{smith2007tesseract,tesseract5} runs with its LSTM recogniser and the Bengali \texttt{tessdata\_best} model, and EasyOCR~1.7.2~\cite{jaidedai2024easyocr} with Bengali enabled. Both use their default settings, which for Tesseract means automatic page segmentation (mode~3), and receive the page image as released (colour scans at 400 dpi, about 1{,}800 by 3{,}000 pixels), without preprocessing. Both ran on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in October 2026. Each engine produces one hypothesis per page. Two engines with different error profiles let us test whether correction behaves differently depending on which engine produced its input.

The engines read the whole page, while the ground truth covers only the annotated text regions. Text outside those regions, such as stamps, shelf marks or handwriting (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}b), therefore counts as inserted characters. The reference joins the region transcriptions in the order in which they appear in the PAGE-XML file; 30 of the 40 files also declare a reading order, and on 17 pages it differs from the file order. The OCR text keeps each engine's own reading order and is not matched to the reference, so a different order also counts as errors. The raw OCR is on average 1.03 (Tesseract) and 1.02 (EasyOCR) times the length of the reference (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}), but this does not show that out-of-region text is negligible, because missing in-region text can offset it. Nor need the mismatch cancel in the paired differences: a model may delete, keep or rewrite out-of-region text differently from the rest. We therefore repeat the main comparison against a reference in the declared reading order, and with a word-recall measure that ignores word order and extra text (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). We did not run a region-level evaluation. OCR output is not stable across installations: an earlier run of Tesseract on the 15-page sample, with an unrecorded Colab build from August 2026, gave different text on all 15 pages (mean CER 0.364 against 0.320), while EasyOCR changed little. All reported pages therefore use one OCR run, and only the decoding ablation uses the August text.

\subsection{Correction layer}
\label{sec:correction}
\textbf{Zero-shot.} Each model is used as released, without training on correction examples, and the main prompt contains no worked examples. The decoder models receive an instruction and the OCR text; BanglaT5 receives the text alone, as detailed below. We chose this setting because we know of no large public set of Bengali OCR error pairs for training, and because it avoids the cost of task-specific training.

\textbf{Prompt.} The decoder models receive the same instruction, through each model's chat template:
\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
The following text was produced by OCR on a historical Bengali document and may contain recognition errors. Correct the OCR errors and output only the corrected Bengali text, with no explanation, preamble, or additional commentary.
\end{quote}
The OCR text follows the instruction. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and has no chat template, so it receives the OCR text alone and is run as a text-to-text model. This prompt was fixed in advance and never revised in response to model output.

\textbf{Prompt variants.} For the four general-purpose models we also test three variants, each changing one thing: the same instruction followed by ``Preserve the historical spelling of the original text; if you are not sure whether something is an error, leave it unchanged''; the instruction translated into Bengali; and the instruction with three worked examples. The examples are single lines from three different development pages, chosen by a fixed rule: the first reference line of 30 to 90 characters whose best-matching OCR line has a CER between 0.05 and 0.30 and contains no Latin letters, taking the engines in turn.

\textbf{Chunking.} Following Kanerva et al.~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, each page is divided into chunks of about 250 tokens of the model's own tokenizer before correction. Chunks are formed by grouping complete lines, so no chunk boundary falls inside a line. Because the tokenizers differ, the resulting chunks range from 168 characters on average for Llama 3.2 1B to 483 for BanglaT5 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). As a model-independent alternative for the four general-purpose models, we also group lines into chunks of at most 300 characters, which every model then receives identically; a single longer line would form its own chunk, but none occurred on our pages. A page whose raw OCR is shorter than ten characters would be left unchanged, since the model would have almost nothing to work from; no evaluation page triggered this rule.

\textbf{Decoding.} Decoding is greedy, so output is deterministic. The maximum number of new tokens is $\min(512,\max(64,1.5n))$, where $n$ is the token length of the input chunk, and an output that reaches this limit is counted as truncated. No other constraint is applied in the main condition. A repetition penalty and a ban on repeated $n$-grams are common defaults against degenerate loops, and we evaluate them as a decoding ablation (\emph{guarded} decoding: penalty 1.3, repeated four-grams banned; Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). In the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers} they are implemented by \texttt{RepetitionPenaltyLogitsProcessor} and \texttt{NoRepeatNGramLogitsProcessor}, which \texttt{generate} applies to the whole token sequence; for a decoder-only model this includes the prompt, and therefore the OCR text being corrected, as the library documents~\cite{hf2026logits}. They penalise every token the model copies from its input and forbid it from reproducing any four-token run of that input. Since correction consists mostly of copying, plain greedy decoding is the main condition. In the library source we inspected (version~5.14.1; the GPU runs used 5.19.0), \texttt{generate} constructs the penalty processor without a prompt length, so the prompt is included; it can be excluded only by constructing the processor by hand.

\subsection{Choice of models}
We evaluate eight models of 0.25 to 3.84 billion parameters (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Parameter counts are computed from each checkpoint's configuration, with tied input and output embeddings counted once.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma-2B-it~\cite{gemma2024} (Gemma~1, 2.51B parameters), Qwen3 1.7B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}, Phi-4-mini~\cite{abouelenin2025phi4mini} and Llama 3.2 1B~\cite{meta2024llama32} are open instruction-tuned multilingual models; Qwen3 and Phi-4-mini were released in 2025. Bengali is a very small share of the pretraining data of general models~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Qwen3 can produce a reasoning trace before its answer; we disable this through its chat template, so it answers directly.
\item \emph{Bengali-pretrained checkpoints.} TituLLMs 1B~\cite{nahin2025titullms} (checkpoint v1.1) is Llama 3.2 1B continually pretrained on Bengali text. It is a base model without instruction tuning and keeps the Llama vocabulary; the template shipped with its tokenizer only concatenates the messages, so it received our instruction and the OCR text as plain text to continue, with no cue for where a corrected version should begin. We also evaluate the instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints at 1B and 3B parameters (v2.0 Instruct), which use an extended vocabulary of 170{,}497 tokens; their model cards give no prompt format, and their shipped template wraps the prompt in an Alpaca-style instruction and response frame, which we used. BanglaT5~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5} (about 248M parameters) is a sequence-to-sequence model pretrained only to restore corrupted text spans and released without task fine-tuning. Without a task prefix it acts as a denoiser rather than a corrector, so we report it as a control. Its model card also asks for inputs passed through the authors' text normaliser, which we did not apply.
\end{itemize}
These results therefore describe these checkpoints with these prompts, not Bengali pretraining in general. BanglaBERT~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} was not considered because it is an encoder and cannot generate corrected text. All models are loaded with the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}.

\subsection{Classical baseline}
To test whether the outcome is specific to language models, we also correct the OCR with a dictionary spell-checker. SymSpell~\cite{garbe2025symspell} replaces every Bengali word that is not in its lexicon with the lexicon word at the smallest edit distance, breaking ties by frequency, if one exists within an edit distance of 2; known words, punctuation, digits and Latin text are left unchanged. The lexicon is the 50{,}000 most frequent words of the Bengali OpenSubtitles corpus~\cite{dave2018frequencywords}, of which 44{,}335 entries consist only of Bengali letters and signs. Its settings are the library defaults, fixed in advance.

\subsection{Computing setting}
The motivating setting is CPU-only use by institutions that process Bengali documents without GPU infrastructure. All the models are small enough to run on a CPU. Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the CPU time per page for the five that were run on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in bfloat16, with plain decoding, on the 15-page sample and its August OCR text, so these times describe a different input from the main runs. To evaluate all 40 pages, eight models and the variants within a free compute budget, the reported runs use a free NVIDIA T4 GPU on Kaggle, in float32, or in float16 for the two models above 3B parameters, which do not fit the GPU's 15~GB in float32. We have not yet rerun those two at higher precision (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). On the pages where a CPU and a GPU run received identical input, the outputs of the general-purpose models were identical or nearly so, and the scores of the other models agreed (Section~\ref{sec:cpugpu}). No paid service is used at any stage.

\subsection{Metrics}
Before scoring, reference and hypothesis are normalised to Unicode NFC and runs of whitespace are collapsed, because Bengali conjuncts and vowel signs can be encoded as different code-point sequences that look identical. Zero-width joiners and non-joiners are kept, and the danda counts as an ordinary character. The character error rate is $\mathrm{CER}=(S+D+I)/N$, where $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the numbers of substituted, deleted and inserted characters in the alignment of the hypothesis with the reference and $N$ is the number of reference characters; the word error rate (WER) is the same quantity over words. Both are computed with jiwer~\cite{vaessen2022jiwer} for each page and averaged over pages, so every page counts equally regardless of its length; the pooled CER divides the total number of character errors by the total reference length. CER counts code points, so an error in one sign of a conjunct counts once although the reader sees the whole cluster as wrong; the grapheme-cluster CER is computed over extended grapheme clusters, which keep a conjunct and its vowel signs together. CER and WER are unbounded, since an output with many insertions can score above 1. We therefore also report the character and word match error rates, $\mathrm{cMER}=(S+D+I)/(H+S+D+I)$ and its word-level counterpart wMER, where $H$ is the number of matched units; both are bounded in $[0,1]$~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Three further normalisations test the scoring choices: mapping ASCII digits to Bengali digits, removing zero-width joiners and non-joiners, and writing khanda-ta as the single code point U+09CE.

\subsection{Statistics}
For each engine and correction method, the unit of analysis is the per-page difference between the corrected and the uncorrected error rate. We report the mean difference with a 95\% percentile interval from a paired bootstrap of the 40 pages (10{,}000 resamples, seed 403), and the number of pages whose error rate fell or rose. We also run a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on the same differences. Pages whose difference is exactly zero, which are common when a method leaves the OCR unchanged, are dropped before ranking, the default of the test, so we report how many pages remain. The primary family is the 36 CER and WER tests (nine methods, two engines, two metrics), adjusted with the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979}, which follows the reporting of Viana~\cite{viana2026reframing}. Every other test in this paper is exploratory: each table that reports one names its own Holm family, and Holm controls the error only within a family. Because pages from the same book are not independent, we repeat the bootstrap resampling whole books instead of pages. For the four general-purpose models, whose claim is that they leave the error rate unchanged, we also test equivalence with two one-sided paired $t$-tests (TOST) against margins of $\pm0.01$ and $\pm0.02$ CER.

The evaluation split was frozen before any model ran, and development pages are never scored. We used this one fixed evaluation set and did not perform cross-validation. No model was trained, and greedy decoding is deterministic, so runs were not repeated with different seeds.

\subsection{Analyses}
\textbf{Sensitivity to the reference.} Two checks address the mismatch between whole-page OCR and region-level ground truth. First, we rebuild each reference with its regions in the reading order declared in the PAGE-XML file, where there is one (regions it does not list follow in file order), and repeat the paired comparison of CER. Second, we compute reference-word recall: the share of reference word tokens that also occur in the output, counted as a multiset. It ignores word order and does not penalise extra words, so neither a different reading order nor OCR text from outside the annotated regions lowers it; it does not reward repairs that leave a word wrong.

\textbf{Clean-text control.} To separate over-correction from the difficulty of noisy input, each model also receives the reference text itself in place of the OCR, with the same prompt and decoding. Any change it makes can then only be damage.

\textbf{Word-level and edit analysis.} We align the ground-truth words with the raw OCR and with the corrected output. A ground-truth word that the raw OCR matched exactly and the output does not is a \emph{broken} word; a word the raw OCR missed and the output matches is a \emph{fixed} word~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. The \emph{change ratio} is the CER of the output measured against the raw OCR, divided by the CER of the raw OCR against the ground truth~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; a ratio above 1 means the model changed more than there was to repair. \emph{Invented runs} are runs of at least six characters that the output contains and the raw OCR does not~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. We also report output length relative to the reference, the share of Bengali, Latin and other characters, ground-truth word retention, edit operations against the reference, the error rate by character class and by page type, and the truncation rate.

\textbf{Output filter not applied.} Kanerva et al.\ strip leading and trailing text that does not align with the input, such as explanatory preambles~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. We did not apply this step, so all scores use unfiltered model output and any preamble or commentary counts as error; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what this means.

\subsection{Safeguards and confidence gating}
\textbf{Page-level safeguards.} As post-hoc checks, we replace a model's output by the raw OCR whenever its length falls outside 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length; these thresholds are taken from a zero-shot system in the HIPE-OCRepair task~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and are not tuned on our pages. A second rule, an edit-distance cap, keeps the output only if its CER measured against the raw OCR is at most $\tau$, for $\tau \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$, a grid fixed in advance. Each safeguarded result is compared with the raw OCR by the same paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon test, with Holm correction over the 18 length-rule tests and, separately, the 72 edit-cap tests.

\textbf{Confidence gating.} Both engines report how confident they are: Tesseract per word (0 to 100) and EasyOCR per detected text box (0 to 1), which every word in the box inherits. We re-ran both engines with the same versions to obtain these values; the re-run reproduced the stored OCR text on all 40 Tesseract pages and 37 EasyOCR pages, and confidences are mapped onto the stored words by word alignment. On the three EasyOCR pages that were not reproduced, as elsewhere, a stored word without a counterpart in the re-run counts as confident, so the gate never lets a model change it. The existing model outputs are then gated: after aligning the OCR words with the output words, an edit that replaces or deletes OCR words is kept only if every OCR word it touches has a confidence below a threshold, and an inserted word only if a neighbouring OCR word does; all other words stay as the OCR read them. The model therefore still corrects whole chunks with their context, but may change only what the engine was unsure of. The thresholds are fixed in advance: 50, 70 and 90 for Tesseract and 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9 for EasyOCR.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The page lists, page-type labels, prompts, few-shot selection rule, and the OCR, correction, baseline, scoring and statistics code are public~\cite{rahman2026repo}, with the frozen split committed before any model was run. Table~\ref{tab:models} names each checkpoint and the revision used; each model's own chat template was used as shipped with that revision, with Qwen3's reasoning trace disabled, and the generation settings are those given under \emph{Decoding} in Section~\ref{sec:correction}. The GPU runs (10--12 October 2026) used Transformers 5.19.0, the OCR used EasyOCR 1.7.2 and Tesseract 5.3.4, and scoring used jiwer 4.0.0 under Python 3.11. The PyTorch and CUDA versions of the Kaggle and Colab images were not recorded. An environment record in the repository lists the known versions, the local analysis environment, and the summary statistics behind every table. Nothing is trained and no paid service is used, so the study can be repeated end to end on free computing resources.

Each comparison pairs a corrected page with its raw OCR. We report the main results and clean-text control, followed by decoding, prompt and chunking checks, comparisons across engines and pages, and statistical robustness.

\subsection{Baseline OCR performance}
EasyOCR has lower error rates than Tesseract on all three baseline metrics (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}); CER is 0.297 rather than 0.341. Tesseract's high error rate is consistent with earlier Bengali evaluations~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr,rabby2024enhancement}, although we found no published comparison of these engines on historical Bengali print. Neither gives clean input for correction: even EasyOCR has WER 0.757.

\subsection{Effect of correction}
The main, ungated methods do not significantly reduce CER for either engine (Table~\ref{tab:matrix} and Fig.~\ref{fig:delta}). Confidence gating gives one small exception, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}. After Holm correction, 23 of the 36 CER/WER comparisons are significant; 22 indicate increased error.

The methods fall into two groups. Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini stay close to the raw OCR: their CER changes lie between $-0.004$ and $+0.017$, and none is significant. Not significant is not the same as unchanged, however (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}). Equivalence within $\pm0.02$ CER is supported only for Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output and Gemma-2B-it on EasyOCR output, and within $\pm0.01$ for none. The other changes are neither significant nor shown to be negligible, and Phi-4-mini raises the bounded wMER significantly on both engines. Each of these models makes some pages better and others worse: Gemma-2B-it 16 better and 16 worse of the 40 Tesseract pages, Qwen3 1.7B 22 and 13. Llama 3.2 1B raises CER slightly but significantly, from 0.341 to 0.380 on Tesseract output and from 0.297 to 0.330 on EasyOCR output ($p=0.002$ and $p=0.017$), and worsens 29 of the 40 pages on each engine. The dictionary baseline raises CER by 0.037 and 0.040 and WER by 0.091 and 0.095 (all $p<0.001$), on 39 and 38 pages. The four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints raise CER by between 0.52 and 1.34 on 38 to 40 pages: BanglaT5 to 0.860 and 0.831, TituLLMs 1B to 1.084 and 1.033, TituLLMs 3B Instruct to 0.958 and 0.886, and TituLLMs 1B Instruct to 1.642 and 1.633. The instruction-tuned 1B checkpoint is therefore worse than the base checkpoint, although the two received differently formatted prompts (Section~\ref{sec:correction}).

The one significant improvement is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, whose WER falls from 0.827 to 0.799 ($-0.028$, 95\% CI $[-0.047,-0.013]$, $p=0.002$). Its grapheme-cluster CER also falls, from 0.431 to 0.417 ($-0.015$, $[-0.032,-0.004]$, $p=0.021$ after Holm correction over 18 tests), but its code-point CER does not change significantly ($-0.004$). Qwen3 thus repairs some whole words on Tesseract text without lowering the overall character error rate; on EasyOCR text it shows no improvement on any metric.

\subsection{Clean-text control}

\subsection{Effect of the decoding setting}
\label{sec:decoding}
Table~\ref{tab:decoding} and Fig.~\ref{fig:decoding} compare guarded decoding, with the repetition penalty and the four-gram ban, against plain greedy decoding. BanglaT5, an encoder-decoder model whose decoder does not see the input tokens as its own history, changed by only 0.011 and 0.020.

Switching OCR engines changes the size of the effect little: for every method, the mean CER deltas differ by at most 0.035. \subsection{Per-page analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:corrected} plots each page's corrected CER against its baseline CER, and Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} the change in CER against the baseline CER. Two patterns appear. For the general-purpose models and the dictionary baseline, the points lie along the identity line and corrected CER follows baseline CER almost exactly (Spearman $\rho$ between 0.88 and 1.00 on both engines): these methods leave most of each page as it was, so the page's own difficulty determines its score. The dictionary baseline shows the same pattern on a smaller scale ($+0.047$ against $+0.021$ on Tesseract output). The changes of the general-purpose models stay within 0.07 of zero in every third. The HIPE-OCRepair task also reports over-correction of low-noise input~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}.

\subsection{Robustness of the statistics and the reference}
\label{sec:robustness}
The conclusions do not depend on how the error is measured, how pages are resampled, or how the reference is ordered (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The pooled CER is lower than the per-page mean (0.298 against 0.341 for raw Tesseract output, 0.286 against 0.297 for EasyOCR), because short pages have higher error rates, but it ranks the methods in the same way, and on it no method is below the raw OCR (Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output: 0.299 against 0.298). Grapheme-cluster CER is higher than code-point CER (0.431 and 0.387 for the raw OCR), because one wrong sign makes a whole conjunct wrong; on this metric only Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output improves, as reported above. On the bounded cMER (Table~\ref{tab:damage}) the baselines are 0.290 and 0.260, and 13 of the 18 tests are significant after Holm correction, all increases; they include Llama 3.2 1B on both engines and Phi-4-mini on EasyOCR output (0.279, $p=0.010$), whose CER changes were smaller. Resampling whole books instead of pages, and grouping pages more coarsely by collection, gives practically the same intervals, as expected with 37 books for 40 pages.

Against a reference rebuilt in the declared reading order, which changes 17 of the 40 references, the raw OCR scores slightly better (CER 0.335 and 0.294 against 0.341 and 0.297), and the same 12 of the 18 CER comparisons are significant, all increases; Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output stays at $-0.004$ ($p=0.82$). Reference-word recall, which ignores word order and extra text, is 0.431 and 0.484 for the raw OCR. No method raises it significantly: Qwen3 1.7B comes closest ($+0.003$ and $+0.002$), and every other method lowers it significantly, Gemma-2B-it included ($-0.017$ and $-0.019$). The absence of a significant improvement therefore persists under the declared reading order and under order-free word recall; a region-matched CER remains untested.

\subsection{CPU and GPU runs}
\label{sec:cpugpu}
On the five pages whose EasyOCR text was byte-identical between the CPU and the GPU runs, Gemma-2B-it produced the same output on all five, and Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B outputs differed from their CPU counterparts by a CER of at most 0.004. The change of hardware alters individual degenerate outputs, not the conclusions. This check is narrow: hardware and precision change together (bfloat16 on the CPU, float32 on the GPU), only five EasyOCR pages qualify, no Tesseract page does because its text changed between the two OCR runs, and the two float16 models were never run on the CPU.

\subsection{Empty outputs and numerical precision}
\label{sec:precision}
TituLLMs 3B Instruct, run in float16, returned an empty page in 43 of its 120 outputs (13 on Tesseract text, 14 on EasyOCR text and 16 in the clean-text control). Each empty page took 0.2 to 0.5~s for all its chunks, against a median of 14.5~s for its non-empty pages, so the model ended generation at or near its first token on every chunk. No output contains the run of identical tokens that greedy decoding produces when the logits become NaN. Phi-4-mini, also in float16, returned no empty output. These observations do not rule out a float16 effect, because overflow could also shift the first-token probabilities towards the end-of-sequence token, and no logits were recorded; until both models are rerun at higher precision, their results, and the empty pages in particular, may partly reflect precision.

\section{Failure Analysis}

Word retention, edit operations, character classes and truncation distinguish the kinds of damage in the outputs. We also check whether page-level safeguards or confidence gating reduce that damage.

\subsection{Word retention and word-level changes}
Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics} reports how many ground-truth words each output retains. Raw Tesseract output keeps 46\% of the distinct ground-truth words and raw EasyOCR output 53\%. Averaged over the outputs of both engines, the general-purpose models keep 46\% to 49\%, the dictionary baseline 37\%, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints between 3\% and 16\%. The script shares in the same table are pooled over all output characters, so they describe the text a method produced, not how often it produced any: the output of TituLLMs 3B Instruct is 97\% Bengali, but 27 of its 80 outputs are empty. The word-level counts in Table~\ref{tab:damage} separate correct words that were changed from wrong words that were fixed. Gemma-2B-it broke 4\% of the words the raw OCR had right and fixed 0.4\% and 0.5\% of those it had wrong. Qwen3 1.7B broke 3\% and 5\% and fixed 3.3\% and 3.5\%, the closest balance of any method. Phi-4-mini and Llama 3.2 1B broke 8\% to 11\%, the dictionary baseline 29\% and 31\%, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints between 74\% and 99\%. The change ratio is below 1 for the general-purpose models and the dictionary (0.1 to 0.4) and between 3.9 and 7.0 for the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints.

Removing the 27 zero-width joiners and non-joiners from the references and outputs changes any method's CER by at most 0.003, and writing khanda-ta as one code point changes nothing, since no text on these pages spells it with a joiner.

\emph{Lexicon coverage.} 52\% of the Bengali word tokens in the references are missing from the 44{,}335-word lexicon of the dictionary baseline. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

For practical use, correction must offer something over retaining raw OCR. Here we consider why it changes correctly recognised text, and why the original decoding settings made that damage look worse.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\textbf{RQ1.} No prompted method significantly reduces per-page CER or bounded cMER on either engine, and none beats raw OCR on pooled CER (Tables~\ref{tab:matrix} and~\ref{tab:robustness}). Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini remain close to the baseline, but equivalence within $\pm0.02$ CER holds only for two of their six cells (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}). Qwen3 1.7B gives the only significant gains, all on Tesseract output and all small: WER falls by 0.028 and grapheme-cluster CER by 0.015, and with confidence gating at one threshold CER falls by 0.004 (Table~\ref{tab:gated}).

\textbf{RQ2.} The methods have similar directions and rankings on the two engines, with CER deltas differing by at most 0.035 (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). This supports consistency across the tested engines, but does not establish equal effect sizes.

\textbf{RQ3.} Few recognition errors are repaired (Table~\ref{tab:damage}). The general-purpose models preserve much of the input, so corrected CER tracks baseline CER page by page (Fig.~\ref{fig:corrected}); they recover a few percent of wrong words but break a similar or larger share of correct ones. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints instead regenerate text: most correct words are lost, and output error rates vary little with input quality. Damage also occurs when the input is already error-free (Table~\ref{tab:clean}).

\textbf{RQ4.} Only decoding changed the conclusion (Table~\ref{tab:decoding}, Fig.~\ref{fig:decoding}): guarded decoding turned Gemma-2B-it's CER change on Tesseract output from none into $+0.22$. Prompt variants and chunking did not bring any model below the raw OCR (Table~\ref{tab:variants}), nor did the page-level safeguards (Table~\ref{tab:safeguards}); confidence gating removed most of the damage but gave no meaningful gain (Table~\ref{tab:gated}).

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Our outcome agrees with reported zero-shot failures on Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches} and Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Noise sensitivity appears even at 70 billion parameters once input CER reaches 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, and HIPE-OCRepair reports over-correction of accurate text~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Successful studies differ in important ways: they fine-tune for correction~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}, use a frontier model on moderately noisy text~\cite{viana2026reframing}, or supply clean English input~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. We combine models of 0.25 to 3.8 billion parameters with no task-specific training, a low-resource non-Latin script, historical spelling and input CER of 0.30 to 0.34. The latter exceeds the 0.25 or less reported in the other studies reviewed here. The supervised Bulgarian result shows that correction can help a non-Latin script~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}, but its language- and engine-specific training differs from our text-only zero-shot setup. The decoding ablation also shows why a negative result needs care: generation settings can make a model that mostly copies appear destructive. Plain decoding should therefore be checked before attributing such failures to correction itself.

\subsection{Possible explanations}
A correction model has to retain most of the input while changing its errors. Our checks help distinguish some reasons for failure, but leave others unresolved.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Input noise.} Noise cannot be the whole explanation: with reference text as input, every method still caused damage, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints reached CERs of 0.83 to 1.57. Three of the four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints also have their smallest CER increase on the hardest pages and their largest on the easiest. We cannot quantify noise's additional contribution from these checks; that would require a controlled noise sweep.
\item \emph{Tokenisation.} Bengali content needs several times the token count of English under English-centric tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer,ahia2023cost}. In our evaluation, tokens per character vary fourfold, from 0.29 to 1.22, and tokens per word from 1.9 to 8.0 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Neither measure gives a simple ranking of outcomes. The TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints have low fertility but high error rates; Llama 3.2 1B and TituLLMs 1B share a tokenizer and the highest fertility, yet one remains near the baseline while the other triples its error rate. Training data and tuning differ too, so tokenizer effects may interact with those differences.
\item \emph{Historical spelling.} A model or lexicon built for modern Bengali may mistake older spellings for errors. Our modern dictionary replaces about 31 correct words per page of clean text. Asking models to preserve spelling slightly reduces damage in seven of the eight general-purpose model-and-engine pairs. Mixing old and modern literary registers also caused difficulty for GPT-4o in grammar correction~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. These observations make historical spelling a plausible explanation, but we have not tested its effect directly.
\item \emph{Prompt format and instruction-following.} The instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints often describe or explain the input, or return nothing, rather than correct it, and the 1B Instruct checkpoint performs worse than its base model. Instruction tuning may not have covered copy-and-edit tasks, but the prompt format is an equally plausible cause: the base checkpoint received our instruction as plain text with no cue for its answer, the Instruct checkpoints an Alpaca-style frame, and BanglaT5 unnormalised text without a task prefix. We did not test other formats, and the empty outputs of TituLLMs 3B Instruct may also reflect its float16 run (Section~\ref{sec:precision}).
\end{itemize}
These explanations and results apply to the checkpoints and configuration tested here. They do not establish how other Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, or fine-tuned versions of these models, would perform.

\subsection{Practical implications}
On this corpus, correction gave no meaningful overall gain in code-point CER, while retaining the raw OCR saves about two to fourteen minutes of CPU processing per page, as measured on the 15-page sample (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The best general-purpose models change CER little; the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints and dictionary increase it. Restricting edits to low-confidence words reduces the measured damage of every method that had increased error, but improves on raw OCR by at most about 1\%. For copy-heavy tasks using Transformers generation, repetition penalties and n-gram bans should be avoided or restricted to generated tokens: their default scope includes the prompt. Fine-tuned small models merit evaluation given results in other languages~\cite{kumar2026postasr,thomas2024leveraging}, but this experiment does not test them.

\subsection{Threats to validity and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Internal validity.} The guarded decoding setting was chosen after seeing development output, and the decoding ablation uses older OCR text on a CPU. The main runs used a GPU in float32, or float16 for Phi-4-mini and TituLLMs 3B Instruct, which have not been checked at higher precision; the motivating setting is a CPU, and on identical inputs the general-purpose models produced identical or nearly identical outputs on both. Each Bengali-pretrained checkpoint received a single prompt format. The few-shot examples come from development pages, which were also used for debugging.
\item \emph{No output filter.} The alignment step that strips preambles was not implemented, so methods that add commentary are penalised for it, the TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints most. Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini add almost none and still do not improve CER, so commentary does not explain the absence of improvement.
\item \emph{Construct validity.} The engines read the whole page while the ground truth covers the annotated regions, and the reference follows the file order of the regions, not the engines' reading order. Out-of-region text and a different order count as errors, and they need not affect raw and corrected text equally. A reference in the declared reading order and an order-free word recall gave the same conclusions, but we did not evaluate region by region. CER weights every code point equally; grapheme-cluster CER, WER and cMER give the same conclusions except for the small gain of Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output.
\item \emph{Statistical validity.} With 40 pages from 37 books the intervals are moderately wide, and small effects such as Llama 3.2 1B's became significant only with 40 pages. The 36 CER and WER tests are the primary family; Holm correction controls the family-wise error within each family, not across them, so the other tests are exploratory.
\item \emph{External validity.} The pages are early printed books, 1713 to 1914, from one release, so the results may not transfer to modern printed Bengali or to other collections. We tested zero-shot use only, models up to 3.8 billion parameters, two open OCR engines, and four prompts. The page types were labelled from the transcriptions by one annotator, without a second annotator, and each type holds only 8 to 13 pages. EasyOCR reports confidence per text box, so gating on its output is coarse.
\item \emph{Experiments not performed.} Five extensions were outside the scope of this zero-shot study on free computing resources: (i) a large open or frontier model on the same pages, which would show how much model scale alone changes the outcome; (ii) fine-tuning, of sequence-to-sequence or byte-level models such as BanglaT5, mT5 or ByT5, or of a small model on synthetic pairs of clean and corrupted text, for which we had neither aligned historical Bengali OCR data nor training compute; (iii) a stronger or cleaner OCR input, which would test whether correction helps once the input CER falls towards the 0.15 or less at which other studies report gains; (iv) direct transcription of the page images by a multimodal model; and (v) a synthetic noise sweep that corrupts the reference at CERs from 0.02 to 0.30 to locate where correction stops helping, of which our clean-text control covers only the noise-free end. The fine-tuning pilot on synthetic pairs is the next experiment we plan.
\item \emph{Scope of the analysis.} Section~V describes how the outputs differ from the input and the reference. It does not apply attribution or other model-internal explanation methods, so the untested explanations in this section are hypotheses.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Zero-shot correction by small open models did not improve historical Bengali OCR in this study, and a common decoding default can make such models look far worse than they are. The general-purpose models mostly copied their input, while the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints we tested regenerated it. For practitioners working under similar conditions, the safe choice is to keep the raw OCR, or to let a copying model change only words the OCR engine marks as uncertain; repetition penalties and $n$-gram bans should not be applied to the prompt. Whether fine-tuning or larger models change this remains to be tested.
