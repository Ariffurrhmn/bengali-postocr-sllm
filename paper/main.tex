\begin{abstract}
Historical Bengali print remains difficult for optical character recognition (OCR), and small language models are a tempting low-cost way to clean its output, but cleaning can quietly damage text that was already right. We measured this on 40 pages printed between 1713 and 1914, read by Tesseract and EasyOCR, with 14 open models of 0.25 to 8 billion parameters prompted without examples, a 32-billion-parameter model on 10 pages, and a small model fine-tuned on synthetic noise. No model significantly reduced the character error rate (CER), on whole pages, on separately read text regions, or on synthetic noise down to a CER of 0.02. The general-purpose models mostly copied their input, as gradient-based attribution maps confirm; larger and fine-tuned models repaired more words but broke others, so CER did not fall. Bengali-pretrained models regenerated the text, and every method changed error-free input. A common decoding default against repetition also penalises the prompt and made copying models appear harmful, raising CER by up to 1.4. Limiting edits to words the engine was unsure of, or combining the two engines, avoided the damage but gained less than 1\%. Archives should keep the raw OCR and never apply repetition penalties to the prompt.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Bengali OCR, post-OCR correction, small language models, zero-shot prompting, historical documents, low-resource NLP, negative results

\section{Introduction}

Bengali has about 278 million speakers and ranks seventh by total speakers~\cite{eberhard2025ethnologue}. Turning its printed records into searchable text remains difficult. The bbOCR evaluation reports an average CER of 0.78 for Tesseract across Bengali document types, compared with 0.59 for the purpose-built bbOCR system~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}. Another study reports Tesseract character accuracy ranging from 37 to 84 percent across document types~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Small differences in strokes distinguish many Bengali characters. Conjunct glyphs combine consonants, and vowel signs can sit before, after, above or below the consonant they modify~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Worn type, uneven inking and ornate display lettering add to the difficulty of historical books; training corpora for recognition are also small~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}.

Post-correction works on the recognised text, so it can be used without modifying or retraining the OCR engine. A text-only correction step could receive output from any engine. Small models are of interest because their lower hardware requirements may suit archives with limited computing resources~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}, including national archives, government offices and non-governmental organisations digitising Bengali documents.

Whether language-model correction helps depends on the setting. Task-specific fine-tuning has improved OCR text~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,beshirov2025bulgarian}, as have large models given nearly clean English input~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. Zero-shot use has produced mixed results: open models helped English but not Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, and only one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models improved an English newspaper corpus~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. Failures have been reported with noisier input and less-resourced languages. Even a 70-billion-parameter model raised error rates once input CER reached about 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; zero-shot models also increased the word error rate of Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. Bengali OCR correction has used lexicons, morphology and edit distance~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}. A commercial language model has also cleaned OCR text from modern textbooks during benchmark construction, without a reference-based check of the result~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. Such cleaning carries a risk: a model can change words that were already right, and an archive that runs one over its scans may make its text worse without knowing it. Evaluations can also mislead in the opposite direction, because a generation setting can make a model that leaves its input alone look destructive. We measure both risks for zero-shot open models, mostly below four billion parameters, on historical Bengali print, with one correction step applied to the output of two OCR engines, and then test why correction fails. We ask:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can open language models of 0.25 to 8 billion parameters, prompted zero-shot, reduce the character and word error rates of OCR output for historical Bengali print?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Is the effect of correction consistent across OCR engines and OCR settings when the correction step is not adapted to either?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} What does correction do to the text: does it repair recognition errors, or does it change text that was already correct?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} Do the decoding setting, the prompt, the chunking or a safeguard change the outcome?
\item \textbf{RQ5.} Is the failure explained by noisy input, by model size or by the lack of task-specific training, and do methods without a language model do better?
\end{itemize}

As prompted, no model significantly reduced CER, on whole pages, on separately read regions, or on synthetic noise at any level. Their outputs ranged from largely unchanged OCR to regenerated text, and all methods altered some correct text. We report the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A decoding pitfall for evaluations of correction: a repetition penalty and an n-gram ban, common defaults against repetition loops, also act on the prompt in a decoder-only model, and they make a model that mostly copies its input appear to damage it. We show this on 40 pages for each constraint alone and both together, including on error-free input.
\item A controlled evaluation of zero-shot post-correction on historical Bengali print: 14 open models of 0.25 to 8 billion parameters and one of 32 billion, two OCR engines at page and region level, and dictionary and engine-fusion baselines, on 40 pages whose split was frozen before any model was run, with paired bootstrap intervals, Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests and equivalence tests.
\item An explanation of the failure: a synthetic noise sweep with no break-even point down to an input CER of 0.02, a model-size series in which larger models repair more words but not more characters, and a fine-tuning pilot in which a small trained model behaves like the larger zero-shot ones.
\item An analysis of what correction changes and whether it can be contained: a clean-text control, correct words broken and wrong words fixed, copying versus regeneration, seen in the outputs and in token-level Grad-CAM maps, precision and prompt-format checks, prompt and chunking variants, page-level safeguards, and edits restricted to words the OCR engine was unsure of.
\item Public code, page lists, prompts, and the per-page OCR text and model outputs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

The relevant work covers recognition, correction of its output, and Bengali models and tokenisation. We also consider grammar and spelling correction in Bengali and other Indic languages.

\subsection{Bengali OCR systems}
Bengali OCR research has mostly addressed recognition itself. bbOCR combines detection, a Bengali recogniser and layout reconstruction to make whole documents searchable; its evaluation compares it only with Tesseract, reporting average text-level CER of 0.59 and 0.78, respectively~\cite{zulkarnain2023bbocr}. Document type matters: Rabby et al.\ train separate word-segmentation models for computer-composed, letterpress, typewritten and handwritten material, obtaining character accuracy of 83 to 90 percent compared with Tesseract's 37 to 84 percent~\cite{rabby2024enhancement}. Other work operates at different scales. Safir et al.\ recognise single handwritten words without surrounding context, with CER 0.091 and WER 0.273~\cite{safir2021endtoend}. Chaudhury et al.\ address degraded printed Bangla by separating text from background with a generative adversarial network, then using an encoder-decoder recogniser designed to reduce word error rate~\cite{chaudhury2022deep}. These approaches improve recognition but leave errors on difficult or degraded pages. None supplies a separate correction step for those errors.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction before language models}
Nguyen et al.'s survey describes post-OCR methods developed before large language models and notes the poorer recognition quality of historical material~\cite{nguyen2021survey}. Shared benchmarks came from the ICDAR competitions: about 12 million characters of English and French monographs and periodicals spanning four centuries in 2017~\cite{chiron2017icdar}, then about 22 million characters in ten European languages in 2019, where the best system improved the text by 44\%~\cite{rigaud2019icdar}. Copying is useful in low-resource correction. A model that can copy characters from first-pass OCR reduced error rates by an average of 34\% across three endangered languages~\cite{rijhwani2020ocr}. Bengali approaches have relied on lexical resources. Pal et al.\ parse words into roots and suffixes for lexicon lookup~\cite{pal2000ocr}; Ahmed et al.\ use edit distance and character n-grams with about 541{,}000 dictionary words, lowering error rates by about 3\% on 50 documents~\cite{ahmed2016improving}. Such methods require aligned OCR/reference pairs or language-specific lexicons. We know of no comparable resource for historical Bengali print, hence our interest in models that require no task-specific training.

\subsection{Post-OCR correction with language models}
Fine-tuning and zero-shot prompting have produced different outcomes. On short segments of 19th-century English newspapers, a fine-tuned Llama 2 reduced CER by 54.5 percent, compared with 23.3 percent for fine-tuned BART~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}. Without task-specific tuning, success varies by language and input quality. In Kanerva et al.'s English and Finnish evaluation, most open-weight models of 8 to 70 billion parameters improved English, but every one made Finnish worse; only GPT-4o, evaluated alongside them, improved Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. Koynov and Doan show how this can change with noise: zero-shot GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.3 70B lowered newspaper CER by 48 to 58 percent when input CER was 0.07, but raised it by about 19 percent at 0.15. Llama also raised German CER by 41 percent at an input CER of 0.25~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. On an English newspaper corpus with input CER 0.084, only one of four quantised 7 to 13 billion parameter models helped; the others increased CER, one five-fold~\cite{vershinin2025lightweight}. A study of instruction-tuned German models likewise found no reduction in average periodical CER, although the best model reduced WER by 22 percent. Its re-analysis of an earlier study found the greatest degradation on inputs longer than about 400 characters~\cite{danilova2025german}. Boros et al.\ evaluated fourteen foundation models, zero- and few-shot, on historical transcripts of several languages, periods and qualities, and concluded that they are ``anything but efficient'' at post-correction~\cite{boros2024postcorrection}. On historical English texts about prosody, GPT models lowered CER by 18.9\% on average in their best setting, but tended to over-correct works whose OCR was already good~\cite{zhang2024prosody}.

The 2026 HIPE-OCRepair task compared zero-shot, fine-tuned and continued-pretraining systems of 8 to 24 billion parameters on English, French and German. The best combined continued pre-training with fine-tuning and outperformed the zero-shot systems; over-correction of accurate text was a recurring failure~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Other successful results use different conditions. A frontier model reduced CER on Portuguese survey pages from 11.7 to 4.7 percent~\cite{viana2026reframing}, while a supervised two-stage system improved historical Bulgarian but was specific to its language and engine~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}. Training data need not be real: a model fine-tuned on text with synthetic character-level corruption lowered CER by 55\% over its base model and outperformed one trained on real OCR pairs~\cite{bourne2024scrambled}. Direct multimodal transcription of 18th-century Russian sometimes beat conventional OCR, yet also introduced archaic characters from the wrong period~\cite{levchenko2025historical}. Modern-language spelling correction can itself be unsuitable for historical text: Do et al.\ attribute part of its poor performance to the mismatch between modern and historical corpora and instead anchor a language model to reference text~\cite{do2025reference}. In the studies reviewed here, zero-shot correction helped mainly with large or frontier models and input CER below about 0.15. Damage to already accurate text remains a common problem.

\subsection{Bengali language models and tokenisation}
Bengali models differ in whether they can generate text at all. BanglaBERT is an encoder for understanding tasks and cannot rewrite OCR output~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}; BanglaT5 is a sequence-to-sequence generation model~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5}. The TituLLMs family uses Bengali-pretrained Llama 3.2 models at 1B and 3B, trained on about 37 billion tokens with an extended tokenizer~\cite{nahin2025titullms}. BanglaLlama continues pretraining other Llama models. Its authors report that Bengali and Hindi together account for less than 0.21 percent of Llama 2's pretraining tokens, compared with 89.7 percent for English~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Limited exposure is only part of the concern. Mahfuz et al.\ find that inefficient Bengali tokenisation increases computation and reduces generation quality when assessing the need for dedicated Bengali models~\cite{mahfuz2025toolate}. Across languages, Petrov et al.\ show that English-centric tokenizers require several times more tokens for equivalent content; Bengali needs roughly five to ten times the English count, compared with about 1.4 to 1.6 times for multilingual XLM-R and M2M100 tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer}. Ahia et al.\ also associate over-segmentation with higher cost and poorer model performance~\cite{ahia2023cost}. General multilingual models therefore face both limited Bengali exposure and inefficient tokenisation. To our knowledge, Bengali-pretrained models have not been evaluated on OCR correction.

\subsection{Correction of Bengali and other Indic text}
The closest Bengali language-model correction study concerns grammar rather than OCR. Instruction-tuning improved its scores by 3 to 7 points over zero-shot use, though humans still did better. Mixing the older literary register with the modern register was among GPT-4o's hardest error categories~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. In Hindi speech-recognition correction, fine-tuned models of 0.3 to 0.58 billion parameters outperformed much larger models. Zero-shot GPT-4o-mini increased WER from 28.6 to 31.8 and from 18.0 to 25.1 percent; the authors attribute this to over-correction that grows with model size~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. For Indic OCR, fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence models improved Sanskrit text, the byte-level ByT5 most~\cite{maheshwari2022sanskrit}, and a fine-tuned model given the surrounding sentence improved Hindi, Gujarati and Marathi OCR~\cite{bhandari2026contextual}. Prompted with retrieved examples, models of 3 to 32 billion parameters improved Hindi and Marathi OCR mainly at the larger sizes; below 8 billion parameters they gained little or made the text worse~\cite{bhandari2026devanagari}. Supervised Bengali spelling systems include BSpell, which combines BERT and a convolutional sub-network and outperforms earlier checkers on two Bengali datasets and one Hindi dataset~\cite{rahman2023bspell}, and a transformer detector-purificator-corrector for Bengali and other resource-scarce Indic languages~\cite{bijoy2025transformer}. BnMMLU provides an example of language-model use after Bengali OCR: GPT-3.5 copy-edited EasyOCR output from modern textbooks and exam guides during dataset preparation. The authors report fewer formatting and spelling errors and manually check about 10\% of the items, but give no reference-based error rate for that step~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. These studies cover modern grammar and spelling, or unmeasured cleaning of modern print. We found no evaluation of language-model OCR correction on historical Bengali print.

\subsection{Research gap}
Table~\ref{tab:literature} compares the language-model studies. Existing Bengali OCR correction uses lexicons, morphology and edit distance~\cite{pal2000ocr,ahmed2016improving}, or a commercial model for cleaning without reference-based evaluation~\cite{joy2026bnmmlu}. None of the reviewed work measures zero-shot language-model correction of historical Bengali print. Among the post-OCR studies in Table~\ref{tab:literature}, only one prompts open models below seven billion parameters~\cite{bhandari2026devanagari}, and none reports an input CER above 0.25, applies one correction step across OCR engines, or tests how decoding settings change the result. Our evaluation addresses these gaps in language, scale, engine coverage and evaluation method.

\section{Methodology}

We score raw and corrected OCR against the same ground truth. The protocol below specifies the corpus, recognition and correction steps, model selection, classical baseline and evaluation.

\subsection{Overview}
Each page passes through two OCR engines, then through a correction model in chunks (Fig.~\ref{fig:method}). We score both versions of the text against its manual transcription. Keeping recognition and correction separate lets the same correction step accept output from any engine. All models are used as released, without training or fine-tuning, except in one fine-tuning experiment (Section~\ref{sec:further}).

\subsection{Dataset}
We use the ground-truth transcriptions released with the ICDAR 2019 Competition on Recognition of Early Indian Printed Documents (REID2019)~\cite{clausner2019reid}, produced by the British Library's Two Centuries of Indian Print project with the PRImA Research Lab and Jadavpur University. We use version~2 of the British Library's release, whose XML coordinates were improved~\cite{britishlibrary2019bengaligt}. The source volumes are Bengali books printed between 1713 and 1914. The digitised images are out of copyright and the transcriptions are released under the Public Domain Mark.

The release contains 81 page images with PAGE-XML annotations. Twenty-nine of them (36\%) have text regions marked but no transcribed text, so no error rate can be computed, and they are excluded; 52 pages remain. We drop the two pages with the thinnest transcriptions, measured by the share of text regions that actually contain text, which leaves a pool of 50 pages. The pool is split into 10 development pages and 40 evaluation pages, and the split was frozen in saved page-identifier lists and committed to version control before any correction model was run. The development pages were used only to debug the pipeline and as the source of the worked examples in one prompt variant; no development page contributes to any reported score. All 40 evaluation pages are scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). They come from 37 books, with at most two pages from any one book (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The release gives no publication date per book. Each page was labelled by its main content, from its transcription, by a single annotator without a second check: 10 title pages, often in ornate display type, 8 verse pages (including verse followed by a commentary), 13 prose pages, and 9 lists (tables of contents, indexes, tables and dictionary entries); the labels are released with the code. A random sample of 15 of the evaluation pages (seed 403) was also processed on a CPU; it provides the CPU timings and an earlier decoding comparison.

\subsection{Baseline OCR}
\label{sec:ocr}
Two open-source engines produce the noisy text. Tesseract~5.3.4~\cite{smith2007tesseract,tesseract5} runs with its LSTM recogniser and the Bengali \texttt{tessdata\_best} model, and EasyOCR~1.7.2~\cite{jaidedai2024easyocr} with Bengali enabled. Both use their default settings, which for Tesseract means automatic page segmentation (mode~3), and receive the page image as released (colour scans at 400 dpi, about 1{,}800 by 3{,}000 pixels), without preprocessing. Both ran on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in October 2026. Each engine produces one hypothesis per page. Two engines with different error profiles let us test whether correction behaves differently depending on which engine produced its input.

The engines read the whole page, while the ground truth covers only the annotated text regions. Text outside those regions, such as stamps, shelf marks or handwriting (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}b), therefore counts as inserted characters. The reference joins the region transcriptions in the order in which they appear in the PAGE-XML file; 30 of the 40 files also declare a reading order, and on 17 pages it differs from the file order. The OCR text keeps each engine's own reading order and is not matched to the reference, so a different order also counts as errors. The raw OCR is on average 1.03 (Tesseract) and 1.02 (EasyOCR) times the length of the reference (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}), but this does not show that out-of-region text is negligible, because missing in-region text can offset it. Nor need the mismatch cancel in the paired differences: a model may delete, keep or rewrite out-of-region text differently from the rest. We therefore repeat the main comparison against a reference in the declared reading order, and with a word-recall measure that ignores word order and extra text (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}), and we repeat the whole evaluation with each region read separately (Section~\ref{sec:further}). OCR output is not stable across installations: an earlier run of Tesseract on the 15-page sample, with an unrecorded Colab build from August 2026, gave different text on all 15 pages (mean CER 0.364 against 0.320), while EasyOCR changed little. Tesseract also drifted within one version: on 17 October 2026, version 5.3.4 with the same model, both on Colab and on Kaggle, no longer reproduced the text of the main run (CER 0.22 between them), which Colab had still reproduced on 12 October. All reported whole-page results therefore use one stored OCR run, which we release (Section~\ref{sec:repro}); only the CPU timings and the earlier decoding comparison on the 15-page sample use the August text.

\subsection{Correction layer}
\label{sec:correction}
\textbf{Zero-shot.} Each model is used as released, without training on correction examples, and the main prompt contains no worked examples. The decoder models receive an instruction and the OCR text; BanglaT5 receives the text alone, as detailed below. We chose this setting because we know of no large public set of Bengali OCR error pairs for training, and because it avoids the cost of task-specific training.

\textbf{Prompt.} The decoder models receive the same instruction, through each model's chat template:
\begin{quote}
\small\itshape
The following text was produced by OCR on a historical Bengali document and may contain recognition errors. Correct the OCR errors and output only the corrected Bengali text, with no explanation, preamble, or additional commentary.
\end{quote}
The OCR text follows the instruction. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and has no chat template, so it receives the OCR text alone and is run as a text-to-text model. This prompt was fixed in advance and never revised in response to model output.

\textbf{Prompt variants.} For the four general-purpose models we also test three variants, each changing one thing: the same instruction followed by ``Preserve the historical spelling of the original text; if you are not sure whether something is an error, leave it unchanged''; the instruction translated into Bengali; and the instruction with three worked examples. The examples are single lines from three different development pages, chosen by a fixed rule: the first reference line of 30 to 90 characters whose best-matching OCR line has a CER between 0.05 and 0.30 and contains no Latin letters, taking the engines in turn.

\textbf{Chunking.} Following Kanerva et al.~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, each page is divided into chunks of about 250 tokens of the model's own tokenizer before correction. Chunks are formed by grouping complete lines, so no chunk boundary falls inside a line. Because the tokenizers differ, the resulting chunks range from 168 characters on average for Llama 3.2 1B to 483 for BanglaT5 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). As a model-independent alternative for the four general-purpose models, we also group lines into chunks of at most 300 characters, which every model then receives identically; a single longer line would form its own chunk, but none occurred on our pages. A page whose raw OCR is shorter than ten characters would be left unchanged, since the model would have almost nothing to work from; no evaluation page triggered this rule.

\textbf{Decoding.} Decoding is greedy, so output is deterministic. The maximum number of new tokens is $\min(512,\max(64,1.5n))$, where $n$ is the token length of the input chunk, and an output that reaches this limit is counted as truncated. No other constraint is applied in the main condition. A repetition penalty and a ban on repeated $n$-grams are common defaults against degenerate loops, and we evaluate them, alone and together, as a decoding ablation (penalty 1.3; repeated four-grams banned; both together are \emph{guarded} decoding; Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). In the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers} they are implemented by \texttt{RepetitionPenaltyLogitsProcessor} and \texttt{NoRepeatNGramLogitsProcessor}, which \texttt{generate} applies to the whole token sequence; for a decoder-only model this includes the prompt, and therefore the OCR text being corrected, as the library documents~\cite{hf2026logits}. They penalise every token the model copies from its input and forbid it from reproducing any four-token run of that input. Since correction consists mostly of copying, plain greedy decoding is the main condition; decoding settings that suit one generation task need not suit another~\cite{wiher2022decoding}. In the library source we inspected (version~5.14.1; the GPU runs used 5.19.0), \texttt{generate} constructs the penalty processor without a prompt length, so the prompt is included; it can be excluded only by constructing the processor by hand.

\subsection{Choice of models}
We evaluate eight models of 0.25 to 3.84 billion parameters (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Parameter counts are computed from each checkpoint's configuration, with tied input and output embeddings counted once.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma-2B-it~\cite{gemma2024} (Gemma~1, 2.51B parameters), Qwen3 1.7B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}, Phi-4-mini~\cite{abouelenin2025phi4mini} and Llama 3.2 1B~\cite{meta2024llama32} are open instruction-tuned multilingual models; Qwen3 and Phi-4-mini were released in 2025. Bengali is a very small share of the pretraining data of general models~\cite{zehady2026banglallama}. Qwen3 can produce a reasoning trace before its answer; we disable this through its chat template, so it answers directly.
\item \emph{Bengali-pretrained checkpoints.} TituLLMs 1B~\cite{nahin2025titullms} (checkpoint v1.1) is Llama 3.2 1B continually pretrained on Bengali text. It is a base model without instruction tuning and keeps the Llama vocabulary; the template shipped with its tokenizer only concatenates the messages, so it received our instruction and the OCR text as plain text to continue, with no cue for where a corrected version should begin. As a check, we also gave it a completion prompt without template, which states that the corrected text follows the OCR text and ends with ``Corrected text:'' (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). We also evaluate the instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints at 1B and 3B parameters (v2.0 Instruct), which use an extended vocabulary of 170{,}497 tokens; their model cards give no prompt format, and their shipped template wraps the prompt in an Alpaca-style instruction and response frame, which we used. BanglaT5~\cite{bhattacharjee2023banglat5} (about 248M parameters) is a sequence-to-sequence model pretrained only to restore corrupted text spans and released without task fine-tuning. Without a task prefix it acts as a denoiser rather than a corrector, so we report it as a control. Its model card also asks for inputs passed through the authors' text normaliser, which the main runs did not apply; a rerun with it is reported in Section~\ref{sec:precision}.
\end{itemize}
These results therefore describe these checkpoints with these prompts, not Bengali pretraining in general. BanglaBERT~\cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} was not considered because it is an encoder and cannot generate corrected text. All models are loaded with the Transformers library~\cite{wolf2020transformers}. Section~\ref{sec:further} adds newer and larger general-purpose models, up to 32 billion parameters, and one fine-tuned model.

\subsection{Classical baseline}
To test whether the outcome is specific to language models, we also correct the OCR with a dictionary spell-checker. SymSpell~\cite{garbe2025symspell} replaces every Bengali word that is not in its lexicon with the lexicon word at the smallest edit distance, breaking ties by frequency, if one exists within an edit distance of 2; known words, punctuation, digits and Latin text are left unchanged. The lexicon is the 50{,}000 most frequent words of the Bengali OpenSubtitles corpus~\cite{dave2018frequencywords}, of which 44{,}335 entries consist only of Bengali letters and signs. Its settings are the library defaults, fixed in advance. Because subtitles are modern and colloquial, we also test a lexicon of the 100{,}000 most frequent words of Bengali Wikipedia~\cite{wikimedia2023wikipedia} (143{,}069 articles), and an edit distance of 1 with each lexicon.

\subsection{Computing setting}
The motivating setting is CPU-only use by institutions that process Bengali documents without GPU infrastructure. All the models are small enough to run on a CPU. Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the CPU time per page for the five that were run on a free Google Colab CPU runtime in bfloat16, with plain decoding, on the 15-page sample and its August OCR text, so these times describe a different input from the main runs. To evaluate all 40 pages, eight models and the variants within a free compute budget, the reported runs use a free NVIDIA T4 GPU on Kaggle, in float32, or in float16 for the two models above 3B parameters, which do not fit the GPU's 15~GB in float32. A float32 rerun on two GPUs, of TituLLMs 3B Instruct on all inputs and of Phi-4-mini on Tesseract text, gave practically the same CER (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). On the pages where a CPU and a GPU run received identical input, the outputs of the general-purpose models were identical or nearly so, and the scores of the other models agreed (Section~\ref{sec:cpugpu}). No paid service is used at any stage.

\subsection{Metrics}
Before scoring, reference and hypothesis are normalised to Unicode NFC and runs of whitespace are collapsed, because Bengali conjuncts and vowel signs can be encoded as different code-point sequences that look identical. Zero-width joiners and non-joiners are kept, and the danda counts as an ordinary character. The character error rate is $\mathrm{CER}=(S+D+I)/N$, where $S$, $D$ and $I$ are the numbers of substituted, deleted and inserted characters in the alignment of the hypothesis with the reference and $N$ is the number of reference characters; the word error rate (WER) is the same quantity over words. Both are computed with jiwer~\cite{vaessen2022jiwer} for each page and averaged over pages, so every page counts equally regardless of its length; the pooled CER divides the total number of character errors by the total reference length. CER counts code points, so an error in one sign of a conjunct counts once although the reader sees the whole cluster as wrong; the grapheme-cluster CER is computed over extended grapheme clusters, which keep a conjunct and its vowel signs together. CER and WER are unbounded, since an output with many insertions can score above 1. We therefore also report the character and word match error rates, $\mathrm{cMER}=(S+D+I)/(H+S+D+I)$ and its word-level counterpart wMER, where $H$ is the number of matched units; both are bounded in $[0,1]$~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. Three further normalisations test the scoring choices: mapping ASCII digits to Bengali digits, removing zero-width joiners and non-joiners, and writing khanda-ta as the single code point U+09CE.

\subsection{Statistics}
For each engine and correction method, the unit of analysis is the per-page difference between the corrected and the uncorrected error rate. We report the mean difference with a 95\% percentile interval from a paired bootstrap of the 40 pages (10{,}000 resamples, seed 403), and the number of pages whose error rate fell or rose. We also run a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} on the same differences. Pages whose difference is exactly zero, which are common when a method leaves the OCR unchanged, are dropped before ranking, the default of the test, so we report how many pages remain. The primary family is the 36 CER and WER tests (nine methods, two engines, two metrics), adjusted with the Holm procedure~\cite{holm1979}, which follows the reporting of Viana~\cite{viana2026reframing}. Every other test in this paper is exploratory: each table that reports one names its own Holm family, and Holm controls the error only within a family. Because pages from the same book are not independent, we repeat the bootstrap resampling whole books instead of pages. For the four general-purpose models, whose claim is that they leave the error rate unchanged, we also test equivalence with two one-sided paired $t$-tests (TOST) against margins of $\pm0.01$ and $\pm0.02$ CER.

The evaluation split was frozen before any model ran, and development pages are never scored. We used this one fixed evaluation set and did not perform cross-validation. No model was trained, and greedy decoding is deterministic, so runs were not repeated with different seeds.

\subsection{Analyses}
\label{sec:analyses}
\textbf{Sensitivity to the reference.} Two checks address the mismatch between whole-page OCR and region-level ground truth. First, we rebuild each reference with its regions in the reading order declared in the PAGE-XML file, where there is one (regions it does not list follow in file order), and repeat the paired comparison of CER. Second, we compute reference-word recall: the share of reference word tokens that also occur in the output, counted as a multiset. It ignores word order and does not penalise extra words, so neither a different reading order nor OCR text from outside the annotated regions lowers it; it does not reward repairs that leave a word wrong.

\textbf{Clean-text control.} To separate over-correction from the difficulty of noisy input, each model also receives the reference text itself in place of the OCR, with the same prompt and decoding. Any change it makes can then only be damage.

\textbf{Word-level and edit analysis.} We align the ground-truth words with the raw OCR and with the corrected output. A ground-truth word that the raw OCR matched exactly and the output does not is a \emph{broken} word; a word the raw OCR missed and the output matches is a \emph{fixed} word~\cite{kumar2026postasr}. The \emph{change ratio} is the CER of the output measured against the raw OCR, divided by the CER of the raw OCR against the ground truth~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}; a ratio above 1 means the model changed more than there was to repair. \emph{Invented runs} are runs of at least six characters that the output contains and the raw OCR does not~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. We also report output length relative to the reference, the share of Bengali, Latin and other characters, ground-truth word retention, edit operations against the reference, the error rate by character class and by page type, and the truncation rate.

\textbf{Attribution.} To see which part of its input each output token relied on, we adapt Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} to token positions. Grad-CAM weights the feature maps of a CNN layer by the gradient of a target score; here the score is the logit of each token the model generated (plain greedy decoding, the main prompt), and the map runs over the token positions of a layer's hidden states. We compute the literal version, which averages the gradient over positions, and an element-wise version, which multiplies gradient and hidden state at each position, as HiResCAM does~\cite{draelos2020hirescam}, at mid depth and at three-quarter depth. The first token, special tokens and the predicting position are left out, because they take a large share of the map without saying anything about the context. For each generated token we record the share of the map on the OCR text, on the rest of the prompt and on the model's own earlier output; for a token that copies an OCR token we record whether the map peaks within one position of that source token. We scored 24 generated tokens in one chunk from each of 10 pages (seed 403), on Tesseract text and on clean text, for all eight models.

\textbf{Output filter not applied.} Kanerva et al.\ strip leading and trailing text that does not align with the input, such as explanatory preambles~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. We did not apply this step, so all scores use unfiltered model output and any preamble or commentary counts as error; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what this means.

\subsection{Safeguards and confidence gating}
\label{sec:gatemethod}
\textbf{Page-level safeguards.} As post-hoc checks, we replace a model's output by the raw OCR whenever its length falls outside 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length; these thresholds are taken from a zero-shot system in the HIPE-OCRepair task~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and are not tuned on our pages. A second rule, an edit-distance cap, keeps the output only if its CER measured against the raw OCR is at most $\tau$, for $\tau \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$, a grid fixed in advance. Each safeguarded result is compared with the raw OCR by the same paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon test, with Holm correction over the 18 length-rule tests and, separately, the 72 edit-cap tests.

\textbf{Confidence gating.} Both engines report how confident they are: Tesseract per word (0 to 100) and EasyOCR per detected text box (0 to 1), which every word in the box inherits. We re-ran both engines with the same versions to obtain these values; the re-run reproduced the stored OCR text on all 40 Tesseract pages and 37 EasyOCR pages, and confidences are mapped onto the stored words by word alignment. On the three EasyOCR pages that were not reproduced, as elsewhere, a stored word without a counterpart in the re-run counts as confident, so the gate never lets a model change it. The existing model outputs are then gated: after aligning the OCR words with the output words, an edit that replaces or deletes OCR words is kept only if every OCR word it touches has a confidence below a threshold, and an inserted word only if a neighbouring OCR word does; all other words stay as the OCR read them. The model therefore still corrects whole chunks with their context, but may change only what the engine was unsure of. The thresholds are fixed in advance: 50, 70 and 90 for Tesseract and 0.5, 0.7 and 0.9 for EasyOCR.

\subsection{Further experiments}
\label{sec:further}
Five further experiments test explanations of the main result: noisy or mismatched input, model size, the lack of training, and the use of a language model at all. Unless stated otherwise they use plain greedy decoding, the main prompt and the main chunking, and each forms its own exploratory Holm family.

\textbf{Region-level OCR.} Each PAGE-XML text region is cropped from the page image (its bounding box plus 8 pixels) and read on its own, Tesseract with page segmentation mode~6, which suits a single block of text. The crop settings were chosen in a comparison on 8 of the evaluation pages with a different Tesseract version (5.5.3), before any model saw region text. The 576 regions with text are joined in reading order and scored against the reference regions in the same order, so out-of-region text and reading-order differences no longer count as errors. Because of the Tesseract drift (Section~\ref{sec:ocr}), whole pages were read again in the same run, and region-level Tesseract output is compared only with that run; EasyOCR reproduced the main run (CER 0.002 between them). All eight models, the dictionary and Qwen3 4B correct the region text.

\textbf{Synthetic noise.} A character-level noise model, estimated from the OCR of the 10 development pages only (substitution, deletion and insertion rates per character, both engines pooled), corrupts the 40 references. One scale factor per level, found by bisection, sets the mean CER to 0.020, 0.048, 0.100, 0.199 and 0.297. The four general-purpose models correct each level, which locates any input CER at which correction starts to help.

\textbf{Newer and larger models.} Qwen3 0.6B, 4B and 8B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}, Gemma~3 1B and 4B~\cite{gemma2025gemma3} (instruction-tuned; the 4B model receives text only) and Llama 3.2 3B Instruct~\cite{meta2024llama32} correct all 40 pages of both engines and the clean text, in float16 above 3 billion parameters and otherwise in float32, except Gemma~3 4B, which overflows in float16 and runs in float32 on two GPUs. Qwen3 32B runs with 4-bit NF4 weights~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} and float16 computation on two T4 GPUs. Because of its cost it corrects only the 10 pages of the attribution analysis, and every other model is rescored on the same 10 pages for comparison.

\textbf{Fine-tuning.} ByT5-small~\cite{xue2022byt5}, a sequence-to-sequence model of about 300 million parameters whose tokens are bytes, so that Bengali needs no special tokenizer, is fine-tuned to turn corrupted text into clean text. ByT5 was the strongest corrector of Sanskrit OCR~\cite{maheshwari2022sanskrit}, and synthetic corruption has replaced aligned OCR data in English~\cite{bourne2024scrambled}, which historical Bengali lacks. The training text is up to 40{,}000 segments of 60 to 300 characters from Bengali Wikipedia, each at least 90\% Bengali letters, plus the reference lines of the 10 development pages, repeated 20 times to add historical spelling. Any segment that shares a string of 25 characters with an evaluation reference is removed. One pair in ten is left clean, so the model also learns to copy; the others are corrupted with the development-page noise model at a CER drawn uniformly from 0.02 to 0.40. Training uses AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, effective batch 8) for 150 minutes on one T4 GPU, and keeps the checkpoint with the lowest CER on the real OCR of the development pages. At test time, chunks of at most 300 characters of whole lines are corrected with their lines joined by spaces, as in training, and greedy decoding. A first run passed the line breaks on to the model, which had never seen one in training; it skipped and repeated whole lines, so we fixed the input format and report the second run.

\textbf{Engine fusion.} A method without a language model combines the two engines word by word. EasyOCR, the engine with the lower CER, is the base text, aligned word by word with Tesseract's. Where the two disagree over at most three words on each side, Tesseract's reading replaces EasyOCR's if its confidence ranks higher within the page; the ranks put the two engines' confidence scales on one footing. A second rule requires a rank at least 0.25 higher. Longer disagreements, and words only one engine read, keep EasyOCR. The confidences are those of the confidence gating (Section~\ref{sec:gatemethod}). Fusion is compared with EasyOCR only, its base; it needs two OCR readings, so it has no clean-text control.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
The page lists, page-type labels, prompts, few-shot selection rule, and the OCR, correction, baseline, scoring and statistics code are public~\cite{rahman2026repo}, with the frozen split committed before any model was run. Table~\ref{tab:models} names each checkpoint and the revision used; each model's own chat template was used as shipped with that revision, with Qwen3's reasoning trace disabled, and the generation settings are those given under \emph{Decoding} in Section~\ref{sec:correction}. The GPU runs (10--12 October 2026) used Transformers 5.19.0, the OCR used EasyOCR 1.7.2 and Tesseract 5.3.4, and scoring used jiwer 4.0.0 under Python 3.11. The PyTorch and CUDA versions of the Kaggle and Colab images used for the main runs were not recorded; all later GPU runs (16 to 18 October 2026, two T4 GPUs) used PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA 12.8 and Transformers 5.19.0, and the dictionary variants and engine fusion ran on a local CPU. An environment record in the repository lists the known versions, the local analysis environment, and the summary statistics behind every table. Because Tesseract's output could not be regenerated a week later (Section~\ref{sec:ocr}), the stored per-page OCR text and all model outputs are released under the CC BY 4.0 licence together with this paper. Apart from the small fine-tuning experiment, nothing is trained, and no paid service is used, so the study can be repeated end to end on free computing resources.

\subsection{Effect of correction}
The main, ungated methods do not significantly reduce CER for either engine (Table~\ref{tab:matrix} and Fig.~\ref{fig:delta}). Confidence gating gives one small exception, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}. After Holm correction, 23 of the 36 CER/WER comparisons are significant; 22 indicate increased error.

The methods fall into two groups. Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini stay close to the raw OCR: their CER changes lie between $-0.004$ and $+0.017$, and none is significant. The one significant improvement among the 36 primary tests is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output, whose WER falls from 0.827 to 0.799 ($-0.028$, 95\% CI $[-0.047,-0.013]$, $p=0.002$). Qwen3 thus repairs some whole words on Tesseract text without lowering the overall character error rate; on EasyOCR text it shows no improvement on any metric.

\subsection{Effect of the decoding setting}
\label{sec:decoding}
Table~\ref{tab:decoding} and Fig.~\ref{fig:decoding} compare plain greedy decoding with the repetition penalty alone, the four-gram ban alone and both together (guarded decoding), for the four general-purpose models on the 40 pages, with the OCR text of both engines and with the clean text as input. Every constraint, alone or combined, raised CER for every model on every input; all 33 comparisons with plain decoding are significant after Holm correction. Guarded decoding raised Gemma-2B-it's CER from 0.353 to 0.545 on Tesseract text, Qwen3 1.7B's from 0.337 to 0.925 and Phi-4-mini's from 0.354 to 1.740, so the three models that leave the OCR almost unchanged under plain decoding become clearly harmful. The clean-text control shows the same effect without any recognition error to repair: guarded decoding raised the CER of error-free input from 0.032 to 0.360 for Gemma-2B-it, from 0.035 to 0.940 for Qwen3 1.7B and from 0.060 to 1.406 for Phi-4-mini. The increase is therefore caused by the decoding constraints, not by the difficulty of the input.

Which constraint does the damage depends on the model. For Gemma-2B-it almost all of it comes from the $n$-gram ban ($+0.176$ on Tesseract text, against $+0.004$ for the penalty). For Phi-4-mini the penalty alone is catastrophic ($+1.396$, with 28 of 40 pages reaching the token limit) and the $n$-gram ban moderate ($+0.155$). For Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B each constraint alone raises CER by 0.35 to 0.49 on OCR text and by 0.59 to 0.69 on clean text. An evaluation that uses either default would thus misjudge a copying model, and testing only one of them would not show the size of the problem for every model. An earlier guarded-versus-plain comparison on the 15-page CPU sample (table note) also included two Bengali-pretrained checkpoints: guarded decoding raised the CER of the decoder-only TituLLMs 1B by more than 1, but not that of BanglaT5, an encoder-decoder model whose decoder does not see the input tokens as its own history.

\subsection{Robustness of the statistics and the reference}
\label{sec:robustness}
The conclusions do not depend on how the error is measured, how pages are resampled, or how the reference is ordered (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The pooled CER is lower than the per-page mean (0.298 against 0.341 for raw Tesseract output, 0.286 against 0.297 for EasyOCR), because short pages have higher error rates, but it ranks the methods in the same way, and on it no method is below the raw OCR (Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output: 0.299 against 0.298). Grapheme-cluster CER is higher than code-point CER (0.431 and 0.387 for the raw OCR), because one wrong sign makes a whole conjunct wrong; on this metric only Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output improves, as reported above. On the bounded cMER (Table~\ref{tab:damage}) the baselines are 0.290 and 0.260, and 13 of the 18 tests are significant after Holm correction, all increases; they include Llama 3.2 1B on both engines and Phi-4-mini on EasyOCR output (0.279, $p=0.010$), whose CER changes were smaller. Resampling whole books instead of pages, and grouping pages more coarsely by collection, gives practically the same intervals, as expected with 37 books for 40 pages.

Against a reference rebuilt in the declared reading order, which changes 17 of the 40 references, the raw OCR scores slightly better (CER 0.335 and 0.294 against 0.341 and 0.297), and the same 12 of the 18 CER comparisons are significant, all increases; Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output stays at $-0.004$ ($p=0.82$). Reference-word recall, which ignores word order and extra text, is 0.431 and 0.484 for the raw OCR. No method raises it significantly: Qwen3 1.7B comes closest ($+0.003$ and $+0.002$), and every other method lowers it significantly, Gemma-2B-it included ($-0.017$ and $-0.019$). The absence of a significant improvement therefore persists under the declared reading order and under order-free word recall, and also when each region is read separately (Section~\ref{sec:region}).

\subsection{CPU and GPU runs}
\label{sec:cpugpu}
On the five pages whose EasyOCR text was byte-identical between the CPU and the GPU runs, Gemma-2B-it produced the same output on all five, and Qwen3 1.7B and Llama 3.2 1B outputs differed from their CPU counterparts by a CER of at most 0.004. The outputs of TituLLMs 1B and BanglaT5 differed more (CER 0.20 and 0.44 between runs), but their scores agreed (0.945 against 0.954 and 0.806 against 0.813). The change of hardware alters individual degenerate outputs, not the conclusions. This check is narrow: hardware and precision change together (bfloat16 on the CPU, float32 on the GPU), only five EasyOCR pages qualify, no Tesseract page does because its text changed between the two OCR runs, and the two float16 models were never run on the CPU.

\subsection{Precision and prompt format}
\label{sec:precision}
Two choices in the main runs could have made the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints look worse than they are: the numerical precision of the two largest models, and the prompt format (Table~\ref{tab:checks}).

\textbf{Precision.} TituLLMs 3B Instruct, run in float16, returned an empty page in 43 of its 120 outputs (13 on Tesseract text, 14 on EasyOCR text and 16 in the clean-text control). Each empty page took 0.2 to 0.5~s for all its chunks, against a median of 14.5~s for its non-empty pages, so the model ended generation at or near its first token on every chunk. Float16 overflow could cause this, so we reran the model in float32 on two GPUs and recorded its first-token logits in both precisions. The first generated token was the same in both precisions on all 208 chunks, and it was the end-of-sequence token on the same 87. No logit or hidden state was infinite or NaN, and the largest activation was about 1{,}000, far below the float16 limit of 65{,}504. The float32 run returned the same empty pages and changed mean CER by at most 0.002 on OCR text (not significant). Phi-4-mini in float32 gave the same CER on Tesseract text (0.354). The empty pages are therefore the model's own behaviour, not a precision artefact.

\textbf{Prompt format.} The base TituLLMs 1B checkpoint received our instruction as plain text with no cue for where its answer should begin. With a completion-style prompt that ends with such a cue, its CER fell from 1.084 to 0.877 on Tesseract text, from 1.033 to 0.861 on EasyOCR text and from 0.988 to 0.605 on the clean text (all $p<0.001$), but it remains far worse than the raw OCR ($+0.54$ and $+0.56$ CER on OCR text). Passing BanglaT5's input through the normaliser its model card asks for changed its CER by at most 0.002. The prompt format thus matters for the base checkpoint, but neither check changes a conclusion: every rerun of a Bengali-pretrained checkpoint stays significantly worse than the raw OCR.

\subsection{Region-level OCR}
\label{sec:region}
Reading each annotated region on its own removes out-of-region text and reading-order differences at the source (Section~\ref{sec:further}), and it lowers the input CER. For EasyOCR, region OCR has CER 0.225 against 0.297 for the whole page (paired difference $-0.072$, 95\% CI $[-0.121,-0.033]$); for Tesseract, with whole pages and regions read by the same build in the same run, 0.333 against 0.425 ($-0.092$, $[-0.152,-0.029]$). Correction still does not help (Table~\ref{tab:region}). No method lowers CER on either engine. Every method is significantly worse on EasyOCR text, and on Tesseract text all except Qwen3 1.7B ($+0.015$, $p=0.063$) and Qwen3 4B ($+0.009$, $p=0.19$). On whole-page EasyOCR output only Llama 3.2 1B among the general-purpose models was significantly worse; on region text all of them are, adding 0.009 to 0.066 CER. With fewer errors to repair, their wrong edits weigh more. The dictionary raises CER by 0.036 and 0.044, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints again regenerate the text (CER 0.82 to 1.63). Qwen3 4B lowers WER while raising CER, on EasyOCR text significantly in both directions ($-0.030$ and $+0.014$).

\subsection{Synthetic noise}
\label{sec:noise}
If the OCR were simply too noisy to correct, correction should start to help as the noise falls. It does not (Table~\ref{tab:noise}, Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}). On references corrupted to mean CERs of 0.020, 0.048, 0.100, 0.199 and 0.297, each of the four general-purpose models raised the CER at every level, and all 20 increases are significant. There is no break-even point: at an input CER of 0.020 the corrected text has CER 0.044 to 0.107, two to five times the input. Gemma-2B-it adds the least, 0.016 to 0.034, and Llama 3.2 1B the most, 0.087 to 0.169. The synthetic noise is not a perfect stand-in for real OCR errors: at the same input CER of about 0.30, the models leave synthetic noise worse than real EasyOCR output (Qwen3 1.7B: 0.362 against 0.315), so the sweep shows the trend, not the exact level a real OCR text would reach.

\subsection{Model size}
\label{sec:scale}
Table~\ref{tab:scale} adds six general-purpose models to the four of the main runs: Qwen3 at 0.6, 4 and 8 billion parameters, Gemma~3 at 1 and 4 billion, and Llama 3.2 3B. None lowers CER on either engine. More parameters do not help consistently within a family: Gemma~3 4B damages more than Gemma~3 1B ($+0.062$ against $+0.022$ on Tesseract text), while Llama 3.2 3B stays close to the raw OCR where Llama 3.2 1B was significantly worse. Qwen3 is the only family in which size moves correction towards improvement. From 0.6B to 8B, the share of wrong words it fixes rises from 4\% to 10\% on Tesseract text and from 4\% to 13\% on EasyOCR text, and WER falls significantly for the 4B and 8B models (by 0.021 to 0.065). \subsection{Fine-tuning}
\label{sec:finetune}
A small model trained for the task behaves much like the larger zero-shot Qwen3 models (Table~\ref{tab:finetune}). The fine-tuned ByT5-small lowers WER significantly on both engines, by 0.024 and 0.037, and on EasyOCR text it fixes 11\% of the wrong words while breaking 7\% of the correct ones. CER does not fall. It rises by 0.012 on Tesseract text, significantly, with 25 of the 40 pages worse, and by 0.016 on EasyOCR text, where the bootstrap interval excludes zero but the Holm-corrected test is not significant. On error-free input it does less damage than any other method run on the 40 pages: CER 0.015, against 0.017 for the best dictionary setting and 0.032 for Gemma-2B-it. The checkpoint chosen on the development pages also stayed just above their raw OCR (CER 0.365 against 0.362). A pilot of 155 minutes and about 14{,}300 training pairs is not the limit of fine-tuning, but it shows that training on synthetic noise teaches the copy-and-edit behaviour that the zero-shot models lack, without making the edits accurate enough to lower CER.

\subsection{Methods without a language model}
\label{sec:classical}
Two further methods use no language model (Table~\ref{tab:classical}). A better lexicon and a smaller edit distance reduce the dictionary's damage but do not remove it. The 100{,}000 most frequent words of Bengali Wikipedia miss 37\% of the reference word tokens, against 52\% for the subtitle lexicon. With this lexicon and an edit distance of 1, SymSpell raises CER by 0.007 and 0.008 instead of 0.037 and 0.040, and error-free text reaches CER 0.017 instead of 0.057. Every setting nevertheless remains significantly worse than the raw OCR on both engines: the best one still breaks 11\% and 13\% of the correct words and fixes 3\% and 8\% of the wrong ones.

Engine fusion combines the two readings instead of correcting one. With EasyOCR as the base and Tesseract's reading taken where its confidence ranks higher, CER falls from 0.297 to 0.294 and 0.295 for the two rules, and WER falls by 0.014 (significant for both). Like the larger Qwen3 models and the fine-tuned model, fusion fixes more wrong words than it breaks correct ones (5.5\% against 3.6\%, and 3.4\% against 1.5\%), and the margin rule breaks fewer correct words than any language model without gating on the 40 pages. Its CER gain is negligible, under 1\%. It is significant only for the margin rule ($-0.002$, $p=0.045$ after Holm correction over the two fusion comparisons), and not when the comparisons with Tesseract output are added to the family ($p=0.09$). We chose the two-comparison family after seeing that result, because a comparison with Tesseract output mostly measures the difference between the engines.

\section{Failure Analysis}

Word retention, edit operations, character classes and truncation distinguish the kinds of damage in the outputs. We also check whether page-level safeguards or confidence gating reduce that damage.

\subsection{Word retention and word-level changes}
Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics} reports how many ground-truth words each output retains. Raw Tesseract output keeps 46\% of the distinct ground-truth words and raw EasyOCR output 53\%. Averaged over the outputs of both engines, the general-purpose models keep 46\% to 49\%, the dictionary baseline 37\%, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints between 3\% and 16\%. The script shares in the same table are pooled over all output characters, so they describe the text a method produced, not how often it produced any: the output of TituLLMs 3B Instruct is 97\% Bengali, but 27 of its 80 outputs are empty. The word-level counts in Table~\ref{tab:damage} separate correct words that were changed from wrong words that were fixed. Gemma-2B-it broke 4\% of the words the raw OCR had right and fixed 0.4\% and 0.5\% of those it had wrong. Qwen3 1.7B broke 3\% and 5\% and fixed 3.3\% and 3.5\%, the closest balance of any method. Phi-4-mini and Llama 3.2 1B broke 8\% to 11\%, the dictionary baseline 29\% and 31\%, and the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints between 74\% and 99\%. The change ratio is below 1 for the general-purpose models and the dictionary (0.1 to 0.4) and between 3.9 and 7.0 for the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints.

\subsection{Edit profiles by model}
Table~\ref{tab:editops} counts substitutions, deletions and insertions per page against the reference; Table~\ref{tab:examples} shows the outputs for two reference lines. In the first example, Gemma-2B-it and Llama 3.2 1B reproduce the Tesseract text unchanged. Qwen3 1.7B repairs a broken word, while the dictionary modernises a historical form. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints return unrelated text or nothing. In the second example, Qwen3 1.7B repairs one word but damages another.

\input{tables/examples}

\emph{General-purpose models.} Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini leave substitutions, deletions and insertions close to the raw OCR's counts (on Tesseract text, Gemma-2B-it: 93, 76 and 64 against 92, 60 and 69). Llama 3.2 1B mainly deletes: deletions rise from 60 to 110 per page on Tesseract text and from 58 to 93 on EasyOCR text.

\emph{Dictionary baseline.} SymSpell adds substitutions (92 to 111 and 84 to 108), because it replaces historical spellings that are missing from its modern lexicon with modern words.

\emph{BanglaT5.} Its output is on average 0.45 times the reference length. Deletions rise to 503 and 434 per page and insertions fall to near zero. More than a quarter (28\%) of its non-space output characters are neither Bengali nor Latin letters, that is, punctuation, ASCII digits and other symbols. BanglaT5 is not instruction-tuned and was not fine-tuned for correction, which may account for this, but we did not test it.

\emph{TituLLMs.} The three checkpoints fail in different ways. The base 1B model substitutes and inserts (370 substitutions and 384 insertions per page on Tesseract text), reached the generation limit on every page, and repeats itself: in 51 of its 80 outputs a segment of at least 20 characters, split at danda, line breaks and vertical bars, recurs three or more times. The 1B Instruct checkpoint produces output 2.09 times the reference length, with 797 and 722 insertions per page; 33\% and 30\% of its output consists of runs of at least six characters absent from the input, it reached the generation limit on 80\% and 92\% of the pages, and it repeats itself in 34 of 80 outputs. The 3B Instruct checkpoint returned an empty page in 27 of its 80 outputs, and in 37 it wrote a Bengali description of the text (for example, that the text appears to be a copy of a historical document) instead of a corrected version; its deletions rise to 432 and 458 per page.

\emph{Character classes.} The raw OCR is least accurate on Bengali digits (65\% and 53\% of reference digits substituted or deleted), on the danda (59\% for Tesseract; EasyOCR never outputs a danda) and on punctuation, and most accurate on consonants (19\% and 14\%). Characters inside conjuncts are wrong in 29\% and 20\% of cases. The general-purpose models raise the error rate of every class slightly; the largest change is Gemma-2B-it on digits (65\% to 75\% and 53\% to 73\%). Most of that change is a script conversion: Gemma-2B-it rewrites Bengali digits as ASCII digits, raising the number of ASCII digits in its output from 88 to 336 on Tesseract text and from 420 to 640 on EasyOCR text, while the references contain 585 Bengali digits and 6 ASCII ones. Because digits are a small share of the text, mapping ASCII digits to Bengali digits before scoring changes its CER by only 0.002 and 0.006. Removing the 27 zero-width joiners and non-joiners from the references and outputs changes any method's CER by at most 0.003, and writing khanda-ta as one code point changes nothing, since no text on these pages spells it with a joiner.

\emph{Lexicon coverage.} 52\% of the Bengali word tokens in the references are missing from the 44{,}335-word lexicon of the dictionary baseline. On pages with more such words, SymSpell breaks a larger share of the words the OCR had right (Spearman $\rho=0.38$ on both engines, $p<0.02$), but its CER on the clean text is unrelated to that share ($\rho=0.08$). A Wikipedia lexicon misses 37\% of them (Section~\ref{sec:classical}).

\emph{Page types.} Lists (contents, indexes, tables) are the hardest pages for both engines, with a raw CER of 0.576 and 0.511, and prose the easiest, at 0.199 and 0.184 (Table~\ref{tab:pagetypes}). No method improves any page type consistently: the mean changes of the general-purpose models lie between $-0.020$ and $+0.066$ in every type, the largest decrease being Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract lists. Three of the four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, and the dictionary baseline, have their smallest increase on lists, the type with the most errors to begin with, which matches the pattern by difficulty in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The groups hold 8 to 13 pages, so these figures are descriptive.

\subsection{What the models attend to}
\label{sec:attribution}
The output analyses above show copying and regeneration from the text alone. Token-level Grad-CAM maps (Section~\ref{sec:analyses}) test the same contrast inside the models, on one chunk from each of 10 pages (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}, Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}). The general-purpose models copy 90\% to 99\% of their output tokens from the input text, and for 58\% to 81\% of the copied tokens the element-wise map at mid depth peaks on the source token itself, against a chance rate of 1\% to 2\%. Their maps put about half of their mass on the input text and most of the rest on their own earlier output. In Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution} this shows as a diagonal: each generated token of Gemma-2B-it relies on the input token it copies. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints copy only 3\% to 25\% of their output tokens, and their maps are spread over the prompt. TituLLMs 1B Instruct relies more on its own earlier output (0.55 and 0.53 of the map) than on the OCR text (0.28 and 0.31), which fits its long, repetitive and invented output described above. The few tokens the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints do copy often peak on their source too (20\% to 68\% at mid depth), so the difference lies mainly in how much they copy, not in how they copy. The maps look much the same with Tesseract text and with clean text as input.

Literal Grad-CAM does not localise: its map peaks on the copied source token for only 0\% to 8\% of copied tokens, close to chance. Averaging the gradient over all positions, as Grad-CAM does for the channels of a CNN, keeps one weight per hidden dimension and discards which position the score depends on; the same weights then highlight every token whose hidden state resembles the source. This is the failure that the element-wise variant was proposed to avoid~\cite{draelos2020hirescam}.

\subsection{Effect of the safeguards}
\label{sec:safeguards}
Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} gives both page-level safeguards with their tests. The length rule replaces outputs only for the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints: BanglaT5 on 21 and 8 of the 40 pages, TituLLMs 3B Instruct on 18 and 23, and TituLLMs 1B Instruct on 5 and 6. This lowers their CER, for example from 0.958 to 0.660 for TituLLMs 3B Instruct on Tesseract output, but every one of them remains significantly worse than the raw OCR (for that example $+0.319$, 95\% CI $[+0.193,+0.460]$, $p=0.001$ after Holm correction over 18 tests). TituLLMs 1B never triggers the rule, because its outputs stay within 0.35 to 2.8 times the input length, and the general-purpose models never trigger it either, so their results are those of the main comparison. The edit-distance cap brings no method significantly below the raw OCR at any $\tau$ in the grid; the lowest is Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output at 0.337 to 0.339 against 0.341, not significant. Seven capped results are significantly worse: the dictionary baseline at $\tau\geq0.1$ on both engines, and Llama 3.2 1B at $\tau=0.3$ on Tesseract output. For the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints the cap rejects all but at most two pages, so their capped output is essentially the raw OCR.

Confidence gating (Table~\ref{tab:gated}) works at the word level instead of the page level. The engines are unsure of much of the text: 41\% of Tesseract words have a confidence below 50 and 77\% below 90, and 52\% of EasyOCR words lie in boxes below 0.5. Gating removes most of the damage of the harmful methods, for example lowering the CER of TituLLMs 1B on Tesseract output from 1.084 to 0.454 and that of the dictionary baseline from 0.378 to 0.348 at the lowest threshold, but nearly all of them remain significantly worse than the raw OCR. For the general-purpose models gating keeps the CER at or just below the raw OCR on Tesseract output. Qwen3 1.7B at threshold 70 gives the only significant reduction of CER by a language model in this study (the other is engine fusion, Section~\ref{sec:classical}), from 0.341 to 0.337 ($-0.004$, 95\% CI $[-0.006,-0.002]$, $p=0.038$ after Holm correction over 54 tests), a relative change of about 1\%. Gating left 4 of the 40 pages identical to the raw OCR; of the 36 non-zero differences the test uses, 25 are improvements and 11 deteriorations. The gain is negligible and not robust: after Holm correction it is not significant at thresholds 50 or 90 (unadjusted $p=0.014$ and 0.069), not on EasyOCR output, and the ungated Qwen3 change it starts from is itself not significant. On EasyOCR output, where confidence is per box and therefore coarse, no gated method improves on the raw OCR. Lower thresholds, which allow fewer changes, give the lowest error throughout.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

For practical use, correction must offer something over retaining raw OCR. Here we consider why it changes correctly recognised text, and why a common decoding default makes that damage look worse.

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\textbf{RQ1.} No prompted model significantly reduces per-page CER or bounded cMER on either engine, and none beats raw OCR on pooled CER (Tables~\ref{tab:matrix} and~\ref{tab:robustness}); this holds for all 14 models of 0.25 to 8 billion parameters (Table~\ref{tab:scale}) and, within its wide intervals, for Qwen3 32B on 10 pages (Table~\ref{tab:large}). Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini remain close to the baseline, but equivalence within $\pm0.02$ CER holds only for two of their six cells (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence}). The gains are in words, not characters: Qwen3 1.7B lowers WER on Tesseract output by 0.028, Qwen3 4B and 8B on both engines by 0.021 to 0.065, and with confidence gating at one threshold Qwen3 1.7B lowers CER by 0.004 (Table~\ref{tab:gated}).

\textbf{RQ2.} The methods have similar directions and rankings on the two engines, with CER deltas differing by at most 0.035 (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). Reading each region separately lowers the input CER of both engines and leaves the conclusion unchanged; the general-purpose models even become significantly harmful on EasyOCR's region text (Table~\ref{tab:region}). This supports consistency across the tested engines and OCR settings, but does not establish equal effect sizes.

\textbf{RQ3.} Few recognition errors are repaired (Table~\ref{tab:damage}). The general-purpose models preserve much of the input, so corrected CER tracks baseline CER page by page (Fig.~\ref{fig:corrected}); they recover a few percent of wrong words but break a similar or larger share of correct ones. Their attribution maps confirm the copying from inside the models: most copied tokens rely most on their own source token (Table~\ref{tab:attribution}, Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}). Larger Qwen3 models fix more wrong words, up to 24\% at 32 billion parameters, but still break correct ones. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints instead regenerate text: they copy few tokens, most correct words are lost, and output error rates vary little with input quality. Damage also occurs when the input is already error-free (Table~\ref{tab:clean}).

\textbf{RQ4.} Only decoding changed the conclusion (Table~\ref{tab:decoding}, Fig.~\ref{fig:decoding}): the repetition penalty, the $n$-gram ban, or both made every general-purpose model significantly harmful, on OCR text and on error-free text alike. A completion prompt lowered the CER of the base TituLLMs 1B checkpoint, and float32 changed nothing, but neither brought a Bengali-pretrained checkpoint near the raw OCR (Table~\ref{tab:checks}). Prompt variants and chunking did not bring any model below the raw OCR (Table~\ref{tab:variants}), nor did the page-level safeguards (Table~\ref{tab:safeguards}); confidence gating removed most of the damage but gave no meaningful gain (Table~\ref{tab:gated}).

\textbf{RQ5.} Input noise does not explain the failure: on synthetic noise, correction raised CER at every level down to an input CER of 0.02 (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}), and on the cleaner region-level OCR it did more harm, not less. Model size helps only at the word level (RQ1). Training helps in the same way: a small byte-level model fine-tuned on synthetic noise lowers WER and barely changes error-free text, but does not lower CER (Table~\ref{tab:finetune}). Without a language model, a better lexicon and a smaller edit distance cut the dictionary's damage by up to four-fifths but leave it significantly harmful, and fusing the two engines breaks few correct words and repairs more than it breaks, but lowers CER by less than 1\% (Table~\ref{tab:classical}).

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Our outcome agrees with reported zero-shot failures on Finnish~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}, on historical transcripts in several languages~\cite{boros2024postcorrection} and on Hindi speech-recognition output~\cite{kumar2026postasr}, and with the finding that models below 8 billion parameters gain little or do harm on Hindi and Marathi OCR even with retrieved examples~\cite{bhandari2026devanagari}. Noise sensitivity appears even at 70 billion parameters once input CER reaches 0.15~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, and over-correction of accurate text has been reported by HIPE-OCRepair~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair} and for GPT models on historical English~\cite{zhang2024prosody}; our clean-text control and noise sweep measure it directly. Successful studies differ in important ways: they fine-tune for correction~\cite{thomas2024leveraging,ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}, use a frontier model on moderately noisy text~\cite{viana2026reframing}, or supply clean English input~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}. We combine models of 0.25 to 8 billion parameters with no task-specific training, a low-resource non-Latin script, historical spelling and input CER of 0.30 to 0.34. The latter exceeds the 0.25 or less reported in the other studies reviewed here, but our region-level OCR (0.23 and 0.33) and synthetic noise down to 0.02 show that the high input CER is not the reason: where a 70-billion-parameter model helped at an input CER of 0.07~\cite{koynov2025opportunities}, small models made even text with a CER of 0.02 worse. The supervised Bulgarian result shows that correction can help a non-Latin script~\cite{beshirov2025bulgarian}, but its language- and engine-specific training differs from our text-only zero-shot setup. The decoding ablation also shows why a negative result needs care: generation settings can make a model that mostly copies appear destructive. Plain decoding should therefore be checked before attributing such failures to correction itself.

\subsection{Possible explanations}
A correction model has to retain most of the input while changing its errors. Our checks help distinguish some reasons for failure, but leave others unresolved.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Input noise.} Noise does not explain the failure. With reference text as input, every method still caused damage; on synthetic noise from CER 0.30 down to 0.02 every general-purpose model raised CER at every level, with no break-even point (Section~\ref{sec:noise}); and region-level OCR, with a lower input CER, was made worse more consistently than whole-page OCR (Section~\ref{sec:region}). Three of the four Bengali-pretrained checkpoints also have their smallest CER increase on the hardest pages and their largest on the easiest. The synthetic noise is only an approximation of real OCR errors, which the models handle somewhat better at the same CER.
\item \emph{Model size.} Within Qwen3, size moves correction towards repairing words: the share of wrong words fixed rises from 4\% at 0.6B to 15\% and 24\% at 32B, and WER falls. CER does not, because the larger models also break correct words and, at 32B, change error-free text more (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Size alone does not make zero-shot correction safe at the character level, at least up to 32 billion parameters in 4-bit.
\item \emph{Task-specific training.} A small model fine-tuned on synthetic noise learned to copy and edit: it changed error-free text least of all methods and lowered WER. It still did not lower CER (Section~\ref{sec:finetune}), so the lack of training explains the regeneration and over-correction of zero-shot models better than it explains the absence of a gain.
\item \emph{Tokenisation.} Bengali content needs several times the token count of English under English-centric tokenizers~\cite{petrov2023tokenizer,ahia2023cost}. In our evaluation, tokens per character vary fourfold, from 0.29 to 1.22, and tokens per word from 1.9 to 8.0 (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Neither measure gives a simple ranking of outcomes. The TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints have low fertility but high error rates; Llama 3.2 1B and TituLLMs 1B share a tokenizer and the highest fertility, yet one remains near the baseline while the other triples its error rate. Training data and tuning differ too, so tokenizer effects may interact with those differences.
\item \emph{Historical spelling.} A model or lexicon built for modern Bengali may mistake older spellings for errors. Our subtitle dictionary replaces about 31 correct words per page of clean text, and half of the reference words are missing from it. A Wikipedia lexicon, which misses about a third of them, together with a smaller edit distance, cuts the dictionary's damage by four-fifths but does not remove it (Section~\ref{sec:classical}). Asking models to preserve spelling slightly reduces damage in seven of the eight general-purpose model-and-engine pairs. Mixing old and modern literary registers also caused difficulty for GPT-4o in grammar correction~\cite{bhattacharyya2025banglagec}. Historical spelling is therefore part of the explanation for the dictionary; for the language models we have not tested its effect directly.
\item \emph{Prompt format and instruction-following.} The instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints often describe or explain the input, or return nothing, rather than correct it, and the 1B Instruct checkpoint performs worse than its base model. The prompt format explains only part of this. A completion prompt with an explicit cue for the answer lowered the base checkpoint's CER by about 0.2 but left it far above the raw OCR, and normalising BanglaT5's input changed nothing (Section~\ref{sec:precision}). The empty outputs of TituLLMs 3B Instruct are not a float16 artefact: the model ends generation at its first token on the same chunks in both precisions. Instruction tuning that did not cover copy-and-edit tasks remains a plausible cause; the Instruct checkpoints, whose model cards give no prompt format, were tested with their shipped template only.
\end{itemize}
These explanations and results apply to the checkpoints and configuration tested here. They do not establish how other Bengali-pretrained checkpoints, or models fine-tuned with more data or with real OCR pairs, would perform.

\subsection{Practical implications}
On this corpus, correction gave no meaningful overall gain in code-point CER, while retaining the raw OCR saves about two to fourteen minutes of CPU processing per page, as measured on the 15-page sample (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The best general-purpose models change CER little; the Bengali-pretrained checkpoints and dictionary increase it. Restricting edits to low-confidence words reduces the measured damage of every method that had increased error, but improves on raw OCR by at most about 1\%. Where both engines are available, fusing them by word confidence needs no model and no GPU, breaks fewer correct words than any ungated language model and repairs more than it breaks, but its CER gain is also below 1\%. Larger models lower WER but not CER, so a project that cares about whole words may consider them, provided it checks their damage to correct text first. For copy-heavy tasks using Transformers generation, repetition penalties and n-gram bans should be avoided or restricted to generated tokens: their default scope includes the prompt.
A small model fine-tuned on synthetic noise is the gentlest corrector we tested, but in our pilot it did not lower CER either; fine-tuning with more data, or with real OCR pairs once they exist, is the most promising next step.

\subsection{Threats to validity and limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Internal validity.} The guarded decoding setting was chosen after seeing development output; the decoding ablation covers the four general-purpose models only (Phi-4-mini not on EasyOCR text). The region crop settings were compared on 8 evaluation pages, with another Tesseract version and before any correction; the test family of the engine fusion was fixed after a first scoring (Section~\ref{sec:classical}). The main runs used a GPU in float32, or float16 for Phi-4-mini and TituLLMs 3B Instruct; a float32 rerun gave the same results, but for Phi-4-mini only on Tesseract text. The motivating setting is a CPU, and on identical inputs the general-purpose models produced identical or nearly identical outputs on both. The instruction-tuned TituLLMs checkpoints received only their shipped prompt format. The few-shot examples come from development pages, which were also used for debugging.
\item \emph{No output filter.} The alignment step that strips preambles was not implemented, so methods that add commentary are penalised for it, the TituLLMs Instruct checkpoints most. Gemma-2B-it, Qwen3 1.7B and Phi-4-mini add almost none and still do not improve CER, so commentary does not explain the absence of improvement.
\item \emph{Construct validity.} The engines read the whole page while the ground truth covers the annotated regions, and the reference follows the file order of the regions, not the engines' reading order. Out-of-region text and a different order count as errors, and they need not affect raw and corrected text equally. A reference in the declared reading order, an order-free word recall and region-level OCR gave the same conclusions; the region-level Tesseract text comes from a later build, whose output differs from the main run. CER weights every code point equally; grapheme-cluster CER, WER and cMER give the same conclusions except for the small gain of Qwen3 1.7B on Tesseract output.
\item \emph{Statistical validity.} With 40 pages from 37 books the intervals are moderately wide, and small effects such as Llama 3.2 1B's became significant only with 40 pages. The 36 CER and WER tests are the primary family; Holm correction controls the family-wise error within each family, not across them, so the other tests are exploratory. The 32-billion-parameter model ran on 10 pages only, too few for any of its comparisons to reach significance.
\item \emph{External validity.} The pages are early printed books, 1713 to 1914, from one release, so the results may not transfer to modern printed Bengali or to other collections. We tested zero-shot use of models up to 8 billion parameters on all pages and of one 32-billion-parameter model on 10 pages, one small fine-tuned model, two open OCR engines, and four prompts. The page types were labelled from the transcriptions by one annotator, without a second annotator, and each type holds only 8 to 13 pages. EasyOCR reports confidence per text box, so gating on its output is coarse.
\item \emph{Experiments not performed.} Four extensions remain outside this study on free computing resources: (i) frontier or larger open models on all 40 pages, beyond the 10-page estimate for 32 billion parameters; (ii) fine-tuning on real, aligned historical Bengali OCR data, which does not exist publicly, or of larger models than the one small pilot; (iii) a stronger OCR engine, or direct transcription of the page images by a multimodal model; and (iv) constrained correction, in which the model only lists word replacements that are then applied to the OCR text, the closest relative of the confidence gating we tested.
\item \emph{Scope of the attribution analysis.} The Grad-CAM maps cover 10 chunks, the eight models of the main runs and two layers. They show which input tokens an output token depends on, not why a model chooses to copy or regenerate, so the explanations in this section remain hypotheses.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Zero-shot correction by open language models did not improve historical Bengali OCR in any setting we tested: whole-page and region-level OCR from two engines, synthetic noise from an input CER of 0.30 down to 0.02, and models of 0.25 to 32 billion parameters. The general-purpose models mostly copied their input, as their attribution maps confirm, and changed a few words in both directions; larger Qwen3 models repaired more words and lowered WER, but not CER, and still changed error-free text. The Bengali-pretrained checkpoints we tested regenerated the text, also at full precision and with a completion prompt. A small byte-level model fine-tuned on synthetic noise lowered WER and barely changed error-free text, but did not lower CER either. A common decoding default makes copying models look far worse than they are: on the same 40 pages it damaged even error-free text.
For practitioners working under similar conditions, the safe choice is to keep the raw OCR; letting a copying model change only words the OCR engine marks as uncertain, or fusing two engines by word confidence, avoids the damage but gains less than 1\% in CER. Repetition penalties and $n$-gram bans should not be applied to the prompt.
